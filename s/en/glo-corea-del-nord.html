North Korea: the people, the history, the regime and the bomb

Updated 20 November 2026

News from North Korea tells of missiles fired into the sea, of military parades and of great-power summits at which its name comes up or, more often, disappears. It rarely explains why a war that began in 1950 has never ended, who really holds power in Pyongyang, and why a poor, isolated and hungry country managed to build the atomic bomb. This profile tries to provide the context. It is updatable: every change is recorded at the bottom, with date and source.

In brief

  • North Korea has about 26 million inhabitants, almost all of them Korean. There are no ethnic or religious divisions: the real divide is political, between those the regime considers loyal and those it does not.
  • Korea was a Japanese colony from 1910 to 1945. At the end of the Second World War it was split in two: the north to the Soviets, the south to the Americans. In 1948 two states were born.
  • The Korean War (1950-1953) ended with an armistice, not a peace. Technically, the two Koreas are still at war.
  • Since 1948 the country has been ruled by the same family: Kim Il Sung, then his son Kim Jong Il, and today his grandson Kim Jong Un. It is one of the most closed and repressive regimes in the world.
  • It is the only state that signed the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons and then withdrew from it (2003). It has carried out six nuclear tests and today is believed to have about 60 warheads.
  • Since 2024 it has been a military ally of Russia and fights alongside it against Ukraine. China remains its main protector.

The country

North Korea occupies the northern half of the Korean peninsula, between China and the Sea of Japan. To the north, the Yalu and Tumen rivers separate it from China; for a few kilometres, at the mouth of the Tumen, it also borders Russia. To the south, the demilitarised zone (DMZ), a strip about four kilometres wide, divides it from South Korea. It is a largely mountainous country: arable plains are few, mostly in the west, which is one reason why food has always been a problem.

The people

The population. The last census, in 2008, counted about 24 million people (2008 census, UN). There has been none since: today estimates put the population at about 26 million (World Factbook, via CountryReports). About 3 million live in Pyongyang, the capital, where living is a privilege granted by the regime (GlobalSecurity).

A warning about the numbers. North Korea publishes very little data, and almost none of it can be verified. The figures in this profile are estimates by international bodies, research centres and foreign intelligence services. Where estimates diverge, we say so.

Koreans — practically everyone. According to the CIA World Factbook the country is ethnically homogeneous, with a small Chinese community and very few Japanese (CountryReports). They speak the same language as South Koreans, written only in the Korean alphabet: in the north almost all words of Chinese, Japanese and Western origin have been removed (Britannica). More than seventy years after the division, north and south use partly different words and accents, but they remain the same people.

The Chinese — a small community, the only minority of any significance (Britannica). There are no reliable public estimates of their number.

The Japanese — very few, according to the same source. They should not be confused with the Japanese citizens abducted by Pyongyang's agents (see "Who intervenes from outside").

The real divide: political "class". In a country without ethnic differences, the regime has created one of its own. Songbun classifies every family according to its ancestors' presumed loyalty to the regime: those descended from anti-Japanese fighters are at the top, those descended from landowners, collaborators or people who fled to the south are at the bottom. On this classification, which is inherited, depend schooling, work, housing and even the right to live in the capital. The UN Commission of Inquiry described it as a form of systematic discrimination (UN Commission of Inquiry, 2014).

Religion. Officially, freedom of worship exists, but in practice organised religion has been almost wiped out. In 2002 the government itself told the UN that 0.16% of the population practised Protestantism, Catholicism, Buddhism or Cheondoism, a religion born in Korea in the nineteenth century. According to one estimate, there may be up to 400,000 underground Christians, who risk the prison camp if discovered (USCIRF, 2026 report). In place of religion there is the cult of the Kims: portraits in every home, statues, anniversaries that mark out the calendar.

Hunger. According to a 2025 UN report, between 2020 and 2022 about 46% of North Koreans were undernourished (Korea Times). The UN human rights office also links this situation to government choices that have worsened access to food.

A brief history

The kingdom and the colony. For more than five centuries, until 1910, the peninsula was a single kingdom, the Joseon dynasty, bound to China by ties of vassalage. In 1910 Japan annexed it (Britannica). For thirty-five years Koreans lived under harsh rule: hundreds of thousands of workers deported to Japan and its other colonies, hundreds of thousands of women forced into sexual slavery in army brothels, the attempt to erase the language and, from 1939, even Korean surnames (History). A resistance emerged, partly armed and partly communist, which also fought from Manchuria. Among its leaders was a young guerrilla: Kim Il Sung.

The division (1945-1948). In August 1945, when Japan surrendered, the United States and the Soviet Union agreed to divide the disarming of Japanese troops along the 38th parallel: the Soviets in the north, the Americans in the south. It was meant to be a temporary line. In 1948 two states were born, each claiming to be the only legitimate one: the Republic of Korea in the south on 15 August, and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea in the north on 9 September, led by Kim Il Sung (Britannica).

The war (1950-1953). On 25 June 1950 the northern army invaded the south. The UN authorised an international force led by the United States, which pushed back the invasion and advanced as far as the Chinese border; China intervened with hundreds of thousands of "volunteers" and drove the Americans back south. The front came to a halt more or less where it had started. Counting the dead, wounded and missing, there were about 4 million casualties, about 2 million of them civilians (Britannica). The north emerged from the war in ruins. The United States dropped 635,000 tonnes of bombs on Korea, more than it had used in the entire Pacific during the Second World War (Time); in Pyongyang a quarter of the buildings were left standing (The National Interest). Northern troops also carried out massacres, and civilians died on both sides. The regime still uses the American bombing today as proof that the country must remain mobilised, and as one of the justifications for its nuclear programme (AP, via The Diplomat). On 27 July 1953 an armistice was signed, not a peace treaty, and South Korean President Syngman Rhee refused to sign it (Britannica). That is why the two Koreas are, technically, still at war.

The reign of Kim Il Sung (1948-1994). Kim Il Sung built a one-party state on the Soviet model, then distanced himself from it with the ideology of juche, "self-reliance". He eliminated internal rivals, collectivised everything and built up a personality cult. In the 1960s and 1970s the north was richer than the south; then the south overtook it and the gap became enormous.

The famine (1994-1998). Kim Il Sung died in 1994 and power passed to his son Kim Jong Il: the first dynastic succession in a communist regime. With the end of the Soviet Union, aid and oil at subsidised prices had come to an end; in 1995 came disastrous floods. The regime calls the famine that followed "the Arduous March". Officially, between 225,000 and 235,000 people died (The National Interest); the most authoritative demographic research puts the figure at 600,000 to 1 million (PIIE), and other estimates go much higher. Kim Jong Il responded with the policy of songun, "military first", and accelerated the nuclear programme.

The bomb (1985-2017). In 1985 North Korea signed the Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT). In 1993 it threatened to withdraw when IAEA inspectors asked to see undeclared sites. In 1994 an agreement with the United States, the Agreed Framework, froze the Yongbyon reactor in exchange for oil and two civilian nuclear power plants (Arms Control Association). The agreement limped on amid mutual accusations of non-compliance and collapsed in 2002: Washington accused Pyongyang of secretly enriching uranium, and the oil deliveries were suspended (Congressional Research Service). That same year Bush had placed North Korea in the "axis of evil". On 10 January 2003 North Korea announced its withdrawal from the NPT: the first state to do so (Arms Control Association). The six-party talks (with China, the United States, Russia, Japan and South Korea) went on intermittently until 2009 without lasting results. Between 2006 and 2017 the country carried out six nuclear tests; the last, in September 2017, was the most powerful.

Kim Jong Un (since 2011). On his father's death, in December 2011, power passed to Kim Jong Un, who was not yet thirty. In 2018-2019 he met Donald Trump three times (Singapore, Hanoi, the DMZ): the Hanoi summit failed on the decisive point, how much of the nuclear programme to dismantle in exchange for how many sanctions lifted. Since then Kim has concentrated on the arsenal.

The turning point (2022-2026). In 2022 a law authorised pre-emptive nuclear strikes and declared the country's nuclear status "irreversible" (Al Jazeera); in 2023 the development of nuclear weapons was written into the Constitution (ConstitutionNet, Reuters). In December 2023 Kim declared that the two Koreas were "two hostile states" and that unification with Seoul could not be achieved (Asia Economy; Yonhap, via Daum); in October 2024 a constitutional revision defined South Korea as a "hostile state" (Military.com, AP), and the regime blew up the roads and railways that linked the two countries. In June 2024 it signed a treaty of mutual military assistance with Russia; since October 2024 its soldiers have been fighting against Ukraine. At the party congress in February 2026 Kim declared the country's nuclear status "irreversible and permanent" (Lowy Institute). In March 2026 parliament approved a new Constitution, whose full text was published on 11 November: it no longer mentions reunification, describes South Korea as a neighbouring country to the south and entrusts command of the nuclear forces to the President of the State Affairs Commission, that is, to Kim (JoongAng Daily).

Who rules and how

North Korea's official name is the Democratic People's Republic of Korea, but it is neither a republic in the usual sense of the word nor a democracy. It is a one-party dictatorship, and in practice a hereditary monarchy: the same family has ruled for almost eighty years.

The party. The Workers' Party of Korea is the only one that counts. Its Congress, convened roughly every five years, sets the political line; the latest, the ninth, was held in February 2026 (KEI). Two other parties exist, but only as a façade.

The state. Kim Jong Un is General Secretary of the party and President of the State Affairs Commission, the body that runs the country. Parliament, the Supreme People's Assembly, meets a few times a year to approve unanimously what has already been decided. In elections there is a single candidate per seat.

Control. In 2014 the UN Commission of Inquiry described a state that "displays many attributes of a totalitarian state" (Helsinki Committee, summary of the report). In 2025 the UN human rights office spoke of "a lost decade": more surveillance, more censorship, more forced labour, and the death penalty extended even to those who distribute foreign films or songs (Jurist). In the political prison camps, the kwanliso, the Commission estimated between 80,000 and 120,000 detainees, often held with their families, where starvation was used as a means of punishment (UPI). The camps still exist, according to Amnesty International (Amnesty).

The economy. It is a state-planned economy, with private markets tolerated on and off. In 2025, according to Human Rights Watch, informal private trade came almost to a complete halt (HRW, World Report 2026). A growing share of revenue comes from activities banned by UN sanctions: up to 100,000 workers sent abroad, mainly to China and Russia, who in 2025 brought in up to 800 million dollars, largely kept by the state (Korea Times); and cryptocurrency theft, at least 1.65 billion dollars between January and September 2025 (Dawn).

Who's who

Kim Jong Un — head of state and party since 2011. Grandson of the founder. He built the nuclear arsenal, met Trump three times and sent soldiers to fight for Russia.

Kim Yo Jong — his sister, a party official. She often speaks for the regime with harsh statements aimed at Seoul and Washington. It was she who described the nuclear programme as "the line from which there is no retreat" (The Quint).

Kim Ju Ae — Kim Jong Un's teenage daughter, increasingly present at official ceremonies, including military parades (Malay Mail). Many observers regard her as the likely heir.

Choe Son Hui — the foreign minister, for many years a negotiator with the United States.

Lee Jae-myung — President of South Korea since June 2025. He favours dialogue with the north: in September 2026 he asked Washington to offer sanctions relief in exchange for a freeze of the nuclear and missile programmes (Arms Control Association).

Where the conflict comes from

A war that never ended. The 1953 armistice stopped the fighting but resolved nothing: not the maritime border, not mutual recognition, not the presence of American troops in the south. For the regime, the American army a few dozen kilometres from Pyongyang is a permanent threat. For Seoul and Washington, so is a North Korean army deployed a few dozen kilometres from Seoul.

Regime survival. For Pyongyang, the bomb is not a weapon to be used but a life insurance policy. The regime is convinced that only nuclear weapons stop the United States from overthrowing it, and the debate among experts on this point is open. Its propaganda has for years cited Saddam Hussein's Iraq and Gaddafi's Libya, two regimes that fell after giving up weapons of mass destruction (AFP, via Inquirer). That is why every proposal for "denuclearisation" is rejected: the regime demands to be recognised as a nuclear state before it will even sit down to negotiate (Arms Control Association).

Two states, one nation. For seventy years both Koreas said they wanted reunification, each on its own terms. Since 2023 the north has stopped even saying so. According to some scholars, defining the south as a "hostile foreign state" also serves to make the use of nuclear weapons against it thinkable, when before it would have been an attack on "compatriots" (Columbia Law School, Korean Legal Studies).

Who benefits

The regime, first of all. The nuclear arsenal guarantees that no one will try to overthrow it by force, and gives it a card to play in every negotiation. The permanent state of war justifies total control over the population and the absolute priority of military spending over food.

China, which above all wants stability. A collapsing North Korea would mean millions of refugees on the border and, perhaps, a united Korea allied with the United States right up against Chinese territory. Beijing prefers an awkward neighbour that is still standing: according to several analysts it has by now accepted North Korea de facto as a nuclear state (Korea Times).

Russia, which since the war in Ukraine has obtained from Pyongyang millions of shells, ballistic missiles and soldiers, and in return offers oil, technology and protection at the Security Council (see "Who intervenes from outside").

Other actors, indirectly. The North Korean threat justifies the American military presence in East Asia, the rearmament of Japan and South Korea and the sale of missile defence systems. This does not mean the threat is invented: it is real. It means that those who denounce it also have something to gain from keeping it centre stage.

Who does not benefit: North Koreans. They pay for the state of war with hunger, with the lack of freedom and, today, with the lives of the soldiers sent to fight in Russia.

Who intervenes from outside

China is North Korea's main trading partner and has been its ally since the 1961 friendship treaty; since 2024 Pyongyang has had a second formal ally, Russia. In June 2026 Xi Jinping went to Pyongyang for the first time in seven years and promised Kim support that "will not change" (Jakarta Post, Reuters). Officially, Beijing has never given up on the denuclearisation of the peninsula, but since November 2025 it no longer mentions it in its official documents (Foreign Policy): for several analysts this is tacit acceptance of the North Korean bomb (Korea Times).

Russia signed a comprehensive strategic partnership treaty with Pyongyang on 19 June 2024. Article 4 commits each of the two countries to provide the other "without delay" with military assistance if it is attacked (Arms Control Association). Since October 2024 North Korean soldiers have been fighting against Ukraine, mainly in Russia's Kursk region. According to South Korean intelligence there were about 11,000 of them, and about half have been killed or wounded (Ukrainska Pravda, citing Yonhap). In 2024 North Korea supplied Russia with millions of shells and more than 100 ballistic missiles, fired at Ukrainian cities; in return it received oil beyond UN limits, air defence systems and data to improve its missiles (MSMT report, UN; Ukrainska Pravda). Moscow maintains that the denuclearisation of North Korea is now "a closed issue" (Reuters, via ThePrint). Pyongyang has admitted sending the soldiers, and in April 2026 it opened a museum dedicated to "overseas military operations" (Ukrainska Pravda).

The United States is a military ally of South Korea and Japan and keeps troops in both countries. From 1958 to 1991 it also kept nuclear weapons in South Korea (Korea Times). Officially it still calls for the "complete denuclearization" of North Korea: it reaffirmed this on 30 October 2026 together with Seoul and Tokyo (Al Jazeera). Trump met Kim three times in 2018-2019 and has since said he wants to see him again; on 19 November 2026 he left China without meeting him (Euronews).

Japan is within range of all North Korean missiles. For decades it has demanded the truth about its citizens abducted by Pyongyang's agents in the 1970s and 1980s: Tokyo recognises 17. In 2002 Kim Jong Il admitted the abductions in front of Prime Minister Koizumi; five abductees returned home, and Japan is still waiting for news of the other twelve (Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Japan).

South Korea lives with its capital some fifty kilometres from the border. Today it is a democracy, but for decades it was ruled by the military: General Park Chung-hee, who came to power in a coup and was backed by the United States (Japan Times), then General Chun Doo-hwan, who in 1980 had the Gwangju uprising bloodily crushed. The first democratic presidential elections came only in 1987, after months of protests (Stanford University). Its attitude towards the north changes a great deal depending on who governs: tougher under conservatives, more open under progressives such as the current president.

International law

The armistice. The 1953 agreement was signed by the UN Command, the North Korean army and the Chinese "volunteers". South Korea did not sign it. There is no peace treaty.

The Non-Proliferation Treaty. North Korea joined it in 1985 and concluded its safeguards agreement with the IAEA only in 1992, six years later (Arms Control Association). Article X of the treaty allows a state to withdraw with three months' notice if "extraordinary events" have jeopardised its supreme interests. Pyongyang announced its withdrawal on 10 January 2003, claiming it took effect the following day; most states consider that it became effective on 10 April 2003, when the three months expired (CNS, Middlebury Institute). Some states still dispute the validity of the withdrawal, because North Korea had already been found in breach of its obligations. It is the only state in the world to have left the treaty.

UN sanctions. From 2006 to 2017 the Security Council adopted, unanimously and with the agreement of China and Russia, some ten resolutions banning North Korea from nuclear and ballistic missile tests, the arms trade, sending workers abroad and most of its exports (CSIS). They are binding on all member states. On 28 March 2024 Russia vetoed the renewal of the UN panel of experts that monitored compliance, which closed on 30 April 2024 (DPPA, UN). Since October 2024 monitoring has been carried out by a group of 11 countries outside the UN, all allies of the United States: the United States, Japan, South Korea, the United Kingdom, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Canada, Australia and New Zealand (Government of the Netherlands). Russia and China are not part of it. By buying weapons from Pyongyang, Russia is violating resolutions it voted for itself.

Crimes against humanity. In 2014 the UN Commission of Inquiry concluded that crimes against humanity had been committed in North Korea "pursuant to policies established at the highest level of the State": extermination, murder, enslavement, torture, rape, forced abortion, persecution (Human Rights Watch). It asked for the case to be referred to the International Criminal Court. North Korea has never joined the Court, and only the Security Council could refer the case: China and Russia have a veto. No one responsible has ever been tried.

The war in Ukraine. By sending soldiers to fight on Russian territory, North Korea is taking part in a war that the UN General Assembly has described as an act of aggression. The treaty with Moscow invokes Article 51 of the UN Charter, self-defence, but the North Korean soldiers were deployed to repel the Ukrainian incursion into Kursk, within a war that began with the Russian invasion.

  • 1950-1953 — Korean War; armistice on 27 July 1953.
  • 1985 — Accession to the Non-Proliferation Treaty.
  • 1994 — Kim Il Sung dies and is succeeded by Kim Jong Il. Agreed Framework with the United States.

  • 2003 — Withdrawal from the Non-Proliferation Treaty; the six-party talks begin.
