The IAEA: what it is, what it does beyond inspections, and who decides when it finds a violation

Updated July 25, 2026

In this site's reporting, the IAEA usually appears in one role only: the body that declares whether Iran has violated its safeguards, or whether a facility is under inspection. That's a real part of its work, but not the only one — and understanding how the Agency is structured also explains why its findings carry so much weight when they land, and so little when someone ignores them.

What it is

The International Atomic Energy Agency grew out of Eisenhower's “Atoms for Peace” address to the UN General Assembly (1953) and formally came into being on 29 July 1957, headquartered in Vienna. It isn't a UN specialized agency in the strict sense: it's an autonomous body with its own statute, but it reports to both the General Assembly and the Security Council — a dual channel rare among international organisations, owed to the nature of its mandate. It now has 181 member states.

A dual mandate, in tension with itself

The statute gives the IAEA two declared tasks from the outset: to accelerate and enlarge the contribution of atomic energy to peace and prosperity, and at the same time to ensure that the assistance it provides is never diverted to military purposes. Promoting and policing aren't roles that add up without friction: the same tension is visible, for instance, in the 123 Agreement between the United States and Saudi Arabia, where the promotional component eroded the verification standard.

How it is organised

The General Conference, made up of all member states, meets once a year to approve the budget and policies. The Board of Governors — 35 states, rotating by region and nuclear relevance — meets about five times a year: it's the body that approves safeguards agreements, elects the Director General and, crucially, is the one that formally declares whether a state is in violation. Since December 2019 the Director General has been Argentina's Rafael Grossi.

What happens after a finding of non-compliance

When the Board of Governors determines that a state is not meeting its obligations, the IAEA has no power to impose sanctions or conduct forced inspections: it can only refer the case to the UN Security Council and the General Assembly. From there, everything depends on the same veto logic already seen for the NPT: the Agency establishes the facts, but doesn't decide the consequences.

Beyond inspections

Most of the IAEA's day-to-day work has nothing to do with non-proliferation at all: it includes nuclear medicine and radiotherapy programmes in low-income countries, isotope techniques for agriculture, civilian plant safety, and the technical response to accidents such as Chernobyl and Fukushima. In 2005 the Agency and its then Director General, Mohamed ElBaradei, received the Nobel Peace Prize.
