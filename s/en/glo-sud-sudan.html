South Sudan: its peoples, its history, who is fighting and why

Updated 25 October 2026

News from South Sudan talks about Kiir and Machar, a treason trial, elections postponed for years, famine and clashes in Jonglei, but rarely explains who the peoples involved are, why a State born to applause in 2011 was at war two years later, and why the peace signed in 2018 never worked. This profile tries to provide the background. It is updatable: every change is recorded at the bottom, with date and source.

In brief

  • South Sudan has about 12.4 million inhabitants, and a very young population: the median age is 19. The largest peoples are the Dinka, between 35 and 40%, and the Nuer, about 15%; dozens of smaller peoples follow, such as the Shilluk, Azande, Bari and Murle.
  • For more than half a century the South fought the governments in Khartoum: two civil wars, from 1955 to 1972 and from 1983 to 2005, the second with about two million dead across Sudan. In 2011, 98.83% voted for independence.
  • In December 2013 the power struggle between President Salva Kiir, a Dinka, and his deputy Riek Machar, a Nuer, became a civil war fought also along ethnic lines. Until 2018 it caused about 383,000 more deaths than normal, half of them violent.
  • The 2018 peace agreement was never really implemented. Machar has been detained since March 2025 and is on trial; in 2026 fighting has resumed, 7.8 million people are going hungry and the government is preparing the first elections in the country's history for 22 December.
  • The country lives on oil: according to the World Bank it accounts for 99% of exports and 85-90% of State revenue. It flows through a pipeline across Sudan, which is at war, and a huge share of that money, as the UN and several research centres document, ends up off-budget.

Land and peoples

South Sudan is landlocked. The oil wells are in the north, in Upper Nile and Unity States (The Conversation, via allAfrica); the south, Equatoria, borders Uganda. Since 2020 the country has been divided into 10 States and 3 "administrative areas" (Ruweng, Greater Pibor and Abyei), after years in which the number of States had been raised first to 28 and then to 32 (Eye Radio).

The population. According to UN estimates reported by Worldometer, in 2026 South Sudan has about 12.4 million inhabitants, with a median age of 19; 28% live in towns. The last census dates back to 2008, when the country was still part of Sudan.

A warning about the numbers. There are no recent, reliable data on ethnic groups. The percentages below are 2011 estimates taken from different sources: read them as orders of magnitude. For the smaller peoples there is no reliable figure, and we say so.

  • Dinka (who call themselves Jieng) — between 35 and 40% of the population (OSAR; CNN, via ABC17). The largest people. President Salva Kiir is a Dinka (OSAR, cit.).
  • Nuer (Naath) — about 15% (same sources). Riek Machar is a Nuer (OSAR, cit.). The so-called "White Army" grew out of the self-defence groups of young Nuer men (see below).
  • Shilluk — the country's third people, with no reliable figure. They live along the Nile and the Sobat, in Upper Nile, and have a hereditary kingdom led by the reth. For years they have accused the Dinka leadership of occupying part of their land; in 2017 an offensive by the government and Dinka militias against the Shilluk armed group Agwelek forced tens of thousands of civilians to flee (Minority Rights Group).
  • Azande, Bari, Kakwa, Murle, Acholi and many others — no reliable figure (OSAR, cit.). Many live in Equatoria, the southern region where the NAS rebel movement operates (see below).

Religion (2020 estimates): Christians 60.5%, traditional religions 32.9%, Muslims 6.2% (CNN, via ABC17). In a country that is overwhelmingly Christian or of traditional religion, religion is not what separates the warring parties.

Oil. South Sudan produces oil in Upper Nile and Unity, but it has no access to the sea: crude reaches the Red Sea through a pipeline to Port Sudan, crossing Sudan, which charges for transit (The Conversation, via allAfrica). According to the World Bank, oil accounts for 99% of exports, 85-90% of State revenue and between 50 and 60% of gross domestic product (World Bank, January 2026); the International Monetary Fund gives similar figures: nearly 90% of fiscal revenue and about 95% of exports (IMF, 2024). In the 2026-27 budget presented in September, oil covers about 79% of expected revenue (The Rio Times). Depending on a single product and a single pipeline makes the State fragile: in February 2024, while fighting raged in Sudan, the pipeline ruptured, production fell from about 150,000 to under 50,000 barrels a day and the economy contracted; exports resumed in April 2025, with new interruptions, and in fiscal year 2025 oil revenue fell by about two thirds (World Bank, cit.).

A short history

The Anglo-Egyptian condominium (1899-1955). Formally Sudan, the South included, is governed jointly by the United Kingdom and Egypt; in practice the British are in charge (Fanack). From 1922 the "closed district ordinances" bar northern Sudanese from entering and trading in the South (Fanack, cit.); from 1930 the "Southern Policy" prepares the South for a separate future, with Christian missionaries instead of an Arab administration (Library of Congress). In the end, however, the South is left inside independent Sudan.

The two wars with Khartoum (1955-2005). In August 1955, five months before Sudan's independence, southern soldiers mutiny: the first civil war begins (Refworld). In 1972 the Addis Ababa Agreement gives the South autonomy, with Juba as capital (CNN, via ABC17). In 1983 President Nimeiri abolishes it and imposes sharia on the whole country; in May of that year John Garang founds the Sudan People's Liberation Army, the SPLA (Refworld, cit.; CNN, cit.). The second war lasts until 2005 and kills about two million people across Sudan (CFR).

The 1991 split. During the war the southern movement splits. In 1991 Riek Machar breaks with Garang: he calls for the South's independence, while Garang wants a united "New Sudan". Machar's forces, helped by Khartoum, attack Bor and kill about 2,000 people, mostly Dinka civilians; Machar publicly apologised in 2012. In 1997 he allies with the Khartoum government, then in 2002 returns to the SPLA (Al Jazeera). The memory of Bor still weighs on relations between Dinka and Nuer.

Peace and independence (2005-2011). On 9 January 2005 the Comprehensive Peace Agreement ends the war and promises the South a referendum within six years. From 9 to 15 January 2011 the South votes: 98.83% choose separation. On 9 July 2011 the Republic of South Sudan is born (CNN, via ABC17). Salva Kiir is president, Riek Machar vice-president (Al Jazeera, cit.).

The civil war (2013-2018). In July 2013 Kiir removes Machar from the government; Machar announces that he will challenge him for the party leadership (Al Jazeera, cit.). On 15 December fighting breaks out in Juba between Dinka and Nuer soldiers; Kiir accuses Machar of an attempted coup, Machar denies it. In the following days hundreds of Nuer men in Juba are rounded up and killed: for the African Union Commission of Inquiry it was "an organised military operation". After Machar flees, his forces in turn commit massacres of Dinka in Bor, Bentiu and Malakal (Al Jazeera, 2015). A peace agreement signed in 2015 collapses in July 2016, when fighting breaks out again in Juba and Machar flees once more; Kiir replaces him with Taban Deng Gai (CNN, via ABC17). Between December 2013 and April 2018 the war causes about 383,000 more deaths than normal, of which about 190,000 violent, according to an estimate by the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine (LSHTM).

Peace on paper (2018-2024). On 12 September 2018 Kiir and Machar sign the Revitalised Agreement (R-ARCSS). In February 2020 the country returns to 10 States (Eye Radio) and a unity government is formed, with Machar as first vice-president (CNN, cit.). The agreement provided for a unified army, a constitution, a census, a war crimes court and elections: in August 2026, 46.4% of its provisions had still not been implemented (analysis on this site). In May 2024 Kenya launches the "Tumaini Initiative" in Nairobi to bring in the groups left out of the agreement; the strongest, Thomas Cirillo's NAS, does not take part (PeaceRep).

War returns (2025-2026). In March 2025 in Nasir, in Upper Nile, the White Army overruns an army base and kills a general; the government arrests ministers and officers close to Machar, and Uganda sends special forces to Juba (Al Jazeera). Machar has been under house arrest since March 2025 and since September 2025 has been on trial for treason, murder and crimes against humanity, charges he denies (International Crisis Group). Since late 2025 there has been fighting in Jonglei between the army and Machar's forces: in the first quarter of 2026 the UN mission counted 767 civilians killed (UNMISS). On 22 September 2026 Kiir dissolves the unity government and calls elections for 22 December (analysis on this site).

Who governs, and how

South Sudan has never held elections since independence: the first are called for 22 December 2026. Salva Kiir has been president since 2011. In its 2026 report, the research centre Freedom House rates the country "not free", with 0 points out of 40 for political rights: elections have been postponed since 2018, the press and civil society are restricted, and the first vice-president has been suspended and put on trial (Freedom House). We explain what distinguishes an autocracy from a democracy in the entry Autocracy.

The succession. Kiir is 74, has governed for about twenty years and has never named a successor. According to the Swiss research centre Small Arms Survey, his method is to build up potential rivals and get rid of them when they show ambition (Small Arms Survey). The latest case is Benjamin Bol Mel: a businessman sanctioned by the United States for corruption since 2017, appointed vice-president in February 2025 and seen as the "crown prince", he was dismissed on 12 November 2025, demoted to private and placed under arrest; meanwhile the weight of Kiir's daughter, Adut Salva Kiir, has grown (Eye Radio; Small Arms Survey, cit.).

Where the conflict comes from

We use the four levels of our piece "Are the world's wars 'Muslim'?". Here religion plays no role: the parties are largely Christian, and the war is fought over power, land and oil money.

The ground: a State born from an army. South Sudan was born after fifty years of war, led by the party and the army that had fought Khartoum, the SPLM and the SPLA, but that had also split internally, as in 1991. It has a single large source of money, oil. And in herding communities the defence of cattle and pastures coexists with raiding and inter-communal violence (allAfrica).

The spark: the 2013 power struggle. The war does not stem from ancient hatred, but from a clash within the ruling party: Kiir removes Machar in July 2013, Machar wants to challenge him, and in December the two camps open fire on each other in Juba (Al Jazeera, cit.).

The flag: ethnic belonging. After the killings of Nuer in Juba and the massacres of Dinka in Bor, Bentiu and Malakal (Al Jazeera, 2015), a clash between two men becomes for many a clash between two peoples. Pospisil notes that many Nuer fighters say they took up arms precisely after the Juba killings (allAfrica). None of these identities makes it lawful to kill a civilian for being Dinka, Nuer or Shilluk.

The external multiplier: the neighbours. Uganda openly defends Kiir. Sudan controls the pipeline on which the State depends, and since 2023 has itself been at war: hundreds of thousands of Sudanese have fled to South Sudan (UNHCR, see below). According to Small Arms Survey, Bol Mel also had business ties with the United Arab Emirates and with Sudan's RSF, who were irritated by his fall (cit.).

Who profits

We document declared or verifiable interests, not motives. If an actor denies, we say so.

Whoever controls the oil. The World Bank lists among the problems of public finances non-transparent oil advances, oil-backed loans and off-budget transactions (World Bank, January 2026). According to an analysis published by The Conversation in 2025, the accounts of the State oil company Nilepet rarely appear in official records and are often managed off-budget, and the fees for protecting the oil fields go directly to the National Security Service, the NSS, without passing through the budget (allAfrica). The "oil for roads" programme paid about 2.2 billion dollars to companies linked to Bol Mel by the end of 2024, according to UN human rights reports cited by Small Arms Survey; almost none of the roads were built (Small Arms Survey).

The president. The dissolution of the unity government leaves all power in Kiir's hands until his successor is sworn in, and some South Sudanese jurists fear the new law will let him postpone the vote again (analysis on this site). The government presents the elections as the fulfilment of the peace agreement.

The armed opposition. The SPLM-IO draws from Machar's trial and from its rejection of the vote a reason not to disarm; local armed groups control territory, pastures and cattle. The opposition too has ordered forced evacuations of civilians in its areas (analysis on this site).

Uganda. Kampala has declared it is defending Kiir's government (Al Jazeera). It also hosts over a million South Sudanese refugees (UNHCR, see below).

Who intervenes from outside

  • Uganda. Special forces in Juba since March 2025 (Al Jazeera).
  • Kenya. Mediating since 2024 through the Tumaini Initiative, at Kiir's invitation (PeaceRep).
  • UN. The UNMISS mission protects civilians and monitors violations (UN). The Security Council maintains an arms embargo, renewed on 29 May 2026 until 31 May 2027 with 9 votes in favour and 6 abstentions, including China and Russia (Petra).
  • Sudan. Controls the pipeline, whose rupture in 2024 caused Juba's revenue to collapse (World Bank, January 2026). The border remains disputed in the Abyei area, rich in oil and claimed by both countries: in September 2026 clashes between Ngok Dinka and Misseriya Arab herders killed at least 26 people; a UN force of about 3,000, UNISFA, operates there (Al Jazeera).
  • United States. Sanctioned Bol Mel for corruption in 2017 (Eye Radio).

The human cost. The 2013-2018 war caused about 383,000 more deaths than normal (LSHTM). Internally displaced people number over 1.9 million (IOM, via ReliefWeb). South Sudanese refugees in neighbouring countries number about 2.48 million: over a million in Uganda, about 690,000 in Sudan, 470,000 in Ethiopia, 215,000 in Kenya (UNHCR). At the same time South Sudan hosts about 660,000 refugees, 95% from Sudan (UNHCR, via The Star). In April 2026, 7.8 million people were in severe food insecurity, more than half the population (analysis on this site).

Documented massacres. We recall some of them, attributed to different parties:

  • Bor, 1991: about 2,000 dead, mostly Dinka civilians, killed by Machar's forces (Al Jazeera).
  • Juba, December 2013: hundreds of Nuer men rounded up and killed by government forces or their allies, in what the African Union Commission calls an organised operation; Bor, Bentiu and Malakal, 2014: revenge attacks on Dinka by Machar's forces, with rapes and killings even in churches and hospitals (Al Jazeera, 2015).
  • Upper Nile, 2017: an offensive by the government and Dinka militias in Shilluk lands, tens of thousands displaced (Minority Rights Group).
  • Jonglei, 2026: 767 civilians killed in the first quarter alone (UNMISS); the order to evacuate Akobo county forced about 270,000 people to flee (analysis on this site).

International law

Treaties. South Sudan:

  • has been a party to the Geneva Conventions and both Additional Protocols since January 2013 (International Commission of Jurists);
  • ratified in 2015 the Convention on the Rights of the Child, the Convention against Torture and the Convention on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women (UN Geneva);
  • is not a party to the Rome Statute: the International Criminal Court can act only through a Security Council referral, which has never happened (analysis on this site).

The missing court. The 2018 peace agreement provided for a hybrid court with the African Union for crimes committed since 2013. It has never been set up (analysis on this site). Machar's defence argues that this court, and not the special court created by the president, should try the charges of crimes against humanity (analysis on this site).

The internal conflict. Common Article 3 of the Geneva Conventions and the Second Protocol apply: they prohibit attacking civilians, summary executions, rape and the use of starvation as a method of warfare. They apply equally to the army, to Machar's forces, to the White Army and to every other armed group.

Missing justice. For the major massacres listed above, the court envisaged by the 2018 peace has never come into being. The most important trial under way is that of the president's main political rival.
