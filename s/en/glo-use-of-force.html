Use of force and self-defence: what the UN Charter prohibits, the two exceptions, and who decides whether an attack was lawful

Updated October 25, 2026

Almost every State that attacks another says it is defending itself. Russia said so in Ukraine in 2022, the United States and Iran said so in the 2026 war, Ethiopia and Eritrea are saying so this year. To understand when the argument holds, you need two articles of the Charter of the United Nations: Article 2, paragraph 4, and Article 51.

What it is

The Charter, signed in 1945, prohibits all States from threatening or using force against the territorial integrity or political independence of another State (Art. 2(4)) (Charter of the United Nations). Before, war was a lawful tool of politics; since then, it no longer is. The prohibition applies even to those who feel threatened or are right in a dispute.

The two exceptions

Authorisation by the Security Council. The Council can determine that there is a threat to the peace or an act of aggression (Art. 39) and, if other measures are not enough, authorise the use of force (Art. 42). It takes nine votes out of fifteen and no vote against from any of the five permanent members: that is why the Security Council rarely authorises force.

Self-defence. Article 51 recognises the right of a State to defend itself, alone or with its allies, "if an armed attack occurs". The response must be necessary and proportionate, must be reported immediately to the Security Council, and lasts only until the Council acts.

What counts as an "armed attack"

The International Court of Justice clarified this in 1986, in the case of Nicaragua v. United States. An armed attack is one of the most grave forms of the use of force: an invasion, or the sending of armed bands with comparable effects. Supplying weapons or support to rebels is unlawful, but it is not an armed attack and does not entitle the victim to respond with war (ICJ, Nicaragua v. United States; extract of the judgment). In 1974 the General Assembly had already defined aggression: invasion, bombardment, blockade of ports, sending armed bands. The list is not closed, and the Security Council decides case by case (resolution 3314).

The grey areas

Defending first. An imminent attack may justify anticipatory self-defence. The most cited test comes from an 1841 letter by US Secretary of State Daniel Webster on the Caroline case: a necessity "instant, overwhelming", leaving no choice of means and no moment for deliberation (Avalon Project, Yale). Preventive war against a future, uncertain threat is something else: for most States and legal scholars it is prohibited.

Armed groups. The Charter was written with States in mind. In 2004 the International Court of Justice ruled out Israel invoking Article 51 for the wall in the West Bank, because the attacks did not come from a foreign State but from territory Israel itself controlled (ICJ, advisory opinion of 9 July 2004). In 2014 the United States struck the Islamic State in Syria, arguing that one may act on the territory of a State that is "unwilling or unable" to stop the attacks (US letter to the Council). It is the position of some States, not a rule accepted by all.

Humanitarian intervention. In 1999 NATO bombed Yugoslavia to stop the repression in Kosovo, without authorisation from the Council. A resolution by Russia, Belarus and India to stop the bombing was rejected by 12 votes to 3 (UN, 26 March 1999). An independent international commission later concluded that the intervention was illegal, though politically and morally justified (Kosovo Commission, 2000). The Charter provides no humanitarian exception.

The limit, in practice

It should be the Security Council that decides whether a war was self-defence. But when the attack comes from one of the five permanent members or one of its allies, the veto blocks everything. Since 17 July 2018 the International Criminal Court can try leaders for the crime of aggression, but for States that are not members of the Court a referral by the Council is needed, that is, once again the consent of the five (Coalition for the ICC). The General Assembly can condemn, not compel.

Cases already cited on this site

  • Russia–Ukraine, 2022. Moscow told the Council it was acting under Article 51 (letter S/2022/154). Ukraine had not attacked Russia; the General Assembly condemned the aggression with 141 votes (What Europe could do).
  • United States–Venezuela, January 2026. The fight against drug trafficking, the American justification, falls under neither of the two exceptions (Interests first, order second).
  • United States, Israel and Iran, 2026. Washington and Tehran both invoked Article 51; a preventive attack on a nuclear programme is the most contested claim (Iran: the unresolved question of the use of force).
  • Ethiopia and Eritrea, October 2026. Each of the two States accuses the other before the Council and invokes the Charter (Ethiopia, a guide to the war).

If every State can call its own war self-defence, the prohibition in Article 2 remains a dead letter. That is why the yardstick must be the same for everyone.
