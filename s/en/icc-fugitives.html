ICC Fugitives

The people the International Criminal Court is looking for, and whom no one has yet handed over.

Last checked: September 27, 2026

When the International Criminal Court issues an arrest warrant, its judges have already examined the Prosecutor’s evidence. They have concluded that there are “reasonable grounds” to believe the person committed genocide, crimes against humanity or war crimes. From that moment, the 125 states that have joined the Court are obliged to arrest that person if they set foot on their territory. But the Court has no police: it depends entirely on the will of governments. This page lists the people for whom that will has, so far, been missing.

We did not choose the names. The list is the Court’s: presidents and militia leaders, Russians and Israelis, Taliban and Sudanese, Libyans and Filipinos. That is exactly why it is a mirror of international justice: it shows to whom the rule applies, and how little it takes to escape it.

How to read this page

  • Everyone listed is presumed innocent until a final conviction (art. 66 of the Rome Statute). A warrant is not a verdict: it is the decision to put someone on trial.
  • We list only the warrants the Court has made public. Others may exist under seal.
  • For each entry we show the public document available: the text of the warrant, often redacted, or only a press release, when the judges keep the warrant confidential to protect witnesses and investigations.
  • The primary source is the Court’s “Defendants” page. The list changes with every arrest, death or withdrawal: the date of the last check is shown at the top.

When states cooperate

Not every warrant stays on paper. Ali Kushayb, a Darfur militia leader, was surrendered in 2020 and sentenced to 20 years in December 2025. The Libyan Khaled El Hishri, arrested in Germany, was surrendered in December 2025 and his trial will open in May 2027. Mahamat Said Abdel Kani, a Séléka commander, was convicted in September 2026. The warrant against Abdallah Banda (Darfur), on the other hand, was vacated on July 23, 2026, after the charges were withdrawn.

Why it concerns Italy too

Italy ratified the Rome Statute in 1999, but has never written into its criminal code the full set of crimes the Court tries. Law 237 of 2012 only governs cooperation with The Hague. In March 2023 the government approved a bill introducing the crime of aggression and extending war crimes, postponing crimes against humanity to a later text; it has not moved since. In July 2025 a more complete proposal (AC 2522, first signatory Laura Boldrini) was tabled in the Chamber of Deputies and assigned to the Justice Committee. The Almasri case shows what this means in practice: a wanted man arrested on Italian soil and sent home.
