Manifesto — Needs first, then the number

This manifesto is not against defence. Ukraine was attacked, it has the right to defend itself, and helping it do so is lawful. Russia spends 7.5% of its GDP on its armed forces. Nobody writing these lines pretends the threat does not exist. It is against something else: the way Europe decided how much to spend, and who will pay. (How this war came about, with the wrongs of all sides, we reconstructed in "From 1990 to 2022".)

A number born in a negotiation

At the summit in The Hague, in June 2025, NATO allies committed to spending 5% of GDP on defence and security by 2035. The number was requested by Washington. The only government that held back, Spain's, argued that 2.1% was enough to meet the capabilities required by the Alliance without cutting social spending; the American president responded by threatening to double tariffs. A month later, the trade deal between the United States and the European Union put 15% tariffs and the purchase of large quantities of American weapons in the same package.

Not everything was imposed: many European governments feared Russia, and feared that the United States would no longer defend them. But the number and the timetable were set this way. NATO's capability targets, to which the number is linked, are classified. No citizen, and almost no parliament, can check whether 5% matches what is needed.

Rules changed in one direction only

For weapons, Europe found fast tools. A hundred and fifty billion in loans approved in ten weeks, through an emergency procedure that left out the European Parliament, so much so that Parliament took the matter to the Court of Justice. An exemption from the Stability Pact that lets states run larger deficits, but only if they spend the money on defence (and, since June 2026, on part of their energy spending). A proposal for the common budget that increases funds for defence and space fivefold.

For schools, healthcare, justice and the upkeep of civilian infrastructure, no exemption. For diplomacy, none either: the share of the European budget devoted to conflict prevention has fallen, and the Union has been discussing for more than a year and a half whether to appoint an envoy for negotiations on Ukraine, without having done so. When needs are equal, the spending with the most convenient rules always wins.

Who has already paid

The bill has started to arrive. The United Kingdom funded higher defence spending by cutting development aid and, in 2026, road and energy projects. Belgium cut its aid by a quarter. Denmark abolished a public holiday. In Italy the Parliamentary Budget Office warns that once the exemption ends the accounts will get harder, because staff and multi-year programmes do not stop. The government says healthcare and schools will not be touched. But today the money is borrowed, and someone will pay the debt back.

And while the percentage is being debated, some try to reach it by changing the labels: calling a bridge or a dam already decided "defence", or hiding military spending among infrastructure. Either way, those who pay no longer know what they are paying for.

Why we call it disproportionate, and dangerous

Disproportionate not because the threat is invented, but because the number comes before the needs. A spending target can be met by buying the wrong things. It can be missed while having what is needed.

Dangerous for two reasons. The first is economic: rearmament paid for with debt and taken from public services weakens the very societies it claims to protect. The second is political: when every alarm has only one ready answer, more weapons, and diplomacy is left without tools, war stops being an exception and becomes a horizon. We have written it on this site before: before any choice of force, it should be possible to show that the negotiating path was exhausted, not abandoned halfway ("We Will Not Look Away While War Is Chosen").

The objections, and our answers

"Without security there is no welfare either." True, and serious economists argue it: a defenceless Europe would attract less investment and grow less. But the argument holds for useful spending, based on needs. It does not say who pays while the growth, if it comes, has not yet arrived.

"Those who criticise rearmament play Moscow's game." No. Asking that spending be justified, that Parliament decide and that diplomacy have its means does not weaken Europe: it makes it more credible. It is spending without a plan, and without consent, that an adversary can exploit.

"Russia spends more than we do." It depends on how you measure it. In dollars Europe spends almost three times as much; at purchasing power parity the gap shrinks a great deal, and some estimates reverse it. That is exactly why a public assessment of needs is required, not a race of percentages.

What we ask

  1. A social clause. No increase in defence spending funded by cuts to healthcare, education, justice and civilian infrastructure. If larger deficits are allowed for weapons, the same rule should protect essential services.
  2. Needs first, then the number. Every government should present to Parliament a public version of the capabilities that are missing and what they cost, and justify spending on that basis, not with a percentage of GDP.
  3. Honest labels. Separate, verifiable accounts for defence and for civil security. No civilian works counted as defence, no military spending hidden in infrastructure.
  4. Parliament decides. No emergency procedures that shut out elected representatives for programmes lasting decades.
  5. Equal standing for diplomacy. Conflict prevention should not be cut more than the rest of the budget, and the Union should appoint an envoy with a mandate for negotiations on Ukraine. What that envoy could bring to the table we proposed in "What Europe could do: seven proposals".
  6. We decide. Defence spending should be set on European needs, not under the threat of tariffs, and should not be tied to trade deals. Purchases should be chosen for what they are for, not for the flag of whoever makes them.

The limit of this manifesto

We do not say how much Europe should spend: we do not have the information to say it, and no citizen does. That is precisely the point. We ask that the number come after the needs, that the needs be explained, that parliaments decide, and that the bill not be paid, in silence, by the school, the hospital and the courthouse down the road.
