The Awá of Arariboia and the duty to protect those who do not want to be found

22 November 2026 — Arariboia Indigenous Land, Maranhão, Brazil

In brief

  • On 16 November 2026 a group of Awá living in voluntary isolation, between 15 and 20 people according to the sources, approached a Funai post and a Guajajara village in the Arariboia Indigenous Land. They asked for yams and went back into the forest.
  • Why they did so is not known. Funai says the reasons are unknown; Survival International links it to the invasions, but says it cannot prove it.
  • Brazil has one of the most advanced systems in the world for protecting isolated peoples, and in 2025 it carried out an operation against invaders in Arariboia ordered by the Supreme Court.
  • For the Inter-American Court, what matters is whether the state took the precautions needed to avoid contact and to keep invaders out. Today there is no evidence to say that Brazil has failed; there is enough to ask it to show the opposite.

On Monday 16 November, in the eastern Brazilian Amazon, a group of people who have no contact with the rest of the world came out of the forest to ask for food. Almost every report gave the same explanation: the forest is being destroyed, the Awá are hungry. It may be true, but no one has proved it. We start from what is known, and from a question that does not depend on the cause: what must a state do for a people that has chosen not to be found?

The facts

The approach. Funai, the federal agency for Indigenous peoples, describes sightings and approaches that began on the morning of the 16th near one of its checkpoints and the Guajajara village of Tawari; according to COAPIMA, the Indigenous organisation of Maranhão, some Awá had already been seen on the 15th (Funai statement, 17/11/2026; Portal Amazônia). The sources do not agree on the number: about 20 according to Survival and Estadão, about 15 according to local media; Funai does not give one. The group, adults and children, asked for yams, received them and went back into the forest (BBC News Brasil, via A Gazeta).

The images. The footage was shot by the Guajajara. Survival released it on 17 November at the request of the Guardians of the Forest, the Guajajara group that patrols the territory (Reuters, via bdnews24). Reuters writes that it could not independently verify the place and date (Reuters, via GV Wire).

The state's response. Funai, the Ministry of Indigenous Peoples and Sesai, the Indigenous health service, have teams on the ground and a situation room to prevent outbreaks of disease. Brazilian policy, the statement recalls, is based on non-contact: the group must be able to remain isolated, if it so chooses, and an occasional approach does not mean that isolation has ended. The main risk is to health: people with little exposure to certain infectious agents can become seriously ill from common diseases (Funai statement).

The Guajajara's criticism. On 18 November the village chiefs criticised the large number of police officers who had entered the reserve, which risks frightening the isolated groups, and reiterated that any contact must come from the Awá alone (Diario de Pernambuco).

The cause: not known. The president of Funai, Lucia Alberta Baré, said that the factors behind the approach are not known (dpa, via Volksstimme). Survival says it cannot establish it, but considers a link with pressure from invaders likely; one of its researchers notes that in November there is usually more game in the forest (Mongabay, via NIT). As of 22 November we found no official data on the group's health. This is not the first contact in the history of the Awá: COAPIMA recalls others in Arariboia up to the 1990s (COAPIMA).

Arariboia: a demarcated land, under siege

Arariboia has been Indigenous land since 1990: more than ten thousand Guajajara and Awá on about 413,000 hectares (Estadão, via O Liberal). The isolated Awá number about eighty; Funai monitors them with a dedicated unit. Inside the land the pressure has been documented for years: large-scale illegal cattle ranching and killings of Guajajara (Mongabay, 2024), and fires that the government itself links to burning for illegal pasture (Agência Gov).

The state has not stood still. In 2025 a federal operation ordered by the Supreme Court (STF) removed illegal occupants and cattle (Agência Gov). On 27 September 2025 the STF closed the case, finding that its objectives had been met, Arariboia included (Lex). Two days later, a Funai mission to the burned areas found recent footprints and traces of the isolated Awá, and Funai opened a situation room for them (Funai, 2025). In autumn 2026 Arariboia again appears among the lands with federal operations against fires (Boletim do Fogo no. 10/2026). Who set them is not stated, and there are no public data on invasions after the operation.

What the law says

The Constitution. Article 231 gives the Union the task of demarcating Indigenous lands and of "protecting them and ensuring respect for all their property"; the peoples have exclusive use of the resources, and any act of occupation is null and void (Federal Constitution). In Arariboia demarcation was completed thirty-six years ago: the outstanding obligation is the other one, protection.

The treaties. ILO Convention 169, law in Brazil since 2004 (Decree 10.088/2019), requires effective protection of lands (Art. 14), penalties and preventive measures against intrusion (Art. 18), protection of the environment (Art. 7) and adequate health services (Art. 25). The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, voted for by Brazil but not binding, calls for mechanisms to prevent any action that dispossesses them of their lands and resources (Art. 8). And the two 1966 UN Covenants, ratified by Brazil, open with the same rule: in no case may a people be deprived of its own means of subsistence.

The two judgments. Brazil has recognised the Inter-American Court of Human Rights since 1998.

  • Xucuru v. Brazil (2018): Brazil's first conviction over the rights of an Indigenous people, for delays in demarcating the land and clearing it of occupants (IACtHR). A demarcated land is not enough if the state does not keep it free.
  • Tagaeri and Taromenane v. Ecuador (judgment of 4 September 2024): the world's first judgment on isolated peoples. Non-contact is the premise; a measure is compliant if the state has taken the precautions needed to avoid contact, and the state must protect these peoples from third parties as well. Ecuador was also found responsible for failing to prevent an attack whose risk it knew of (IACtHR, press release 19/2025).

Protection on paper and protection on the ground

Brazil has good arguments: a non-contact policy since 1987, a Funai unit for isolated peoples, a Supreme Court that ordered and verified the operations. In October INPE recorded the lowest deforestation in the Amazon since 1988 (INPE/MMA). But a national figure says nothing about a single territory, and for a people of eighty, the single territory is what counts.

The Tagaeri yardstick asks whether the precautions were sufficient. On this the data are missing: we do not know why the Awá came out, how they are, how many invaders have returned. We do know, however, that the risk was known. In the Supreme Court case Arariboia was among the seven most critical Indigenous lands in the country (Casa de Governo, 2025), and in 2025 Funai had already opened a situation room for the fires near the isolated groups. And we know that the signal has arrived.

The Court did not write that in such cases it is for the state to prove it has done enough: that is our reading. But it is the practical consequence. If compliance is measured by the precautions taken against a known risk, whoever knew the risk is whoever has the data to show them. The Awá cannot say; from outside it cannot be verified.

There is a second side, the one the Guajajara were the first to put on the table: non-contact applies to the state too. Protecting, here, means keeping others out without going in.

Symmetry test

The yardstick. If another state, European or not, failed to stop private actors from taking away the livelihood of a protected minority, this site would not speak of genocide or of a war crime: those require intent and, in the second case, an armed conflict. It would speak of a duty of protection possibly not fulfilled, and it would ask for evidence. It is the same yardstick we use here.

The sources. We do not accept Survival's explanation just because it is plausible: until there are data it is a hypothesis, and Survival presents it as such. Nor do we accept the government's line that everything follows the protocols: it has to be verified. And the record deforestation figures, presented between the two rounds of the presidential election won by Lula, are reliable but aggregated: neither the government nor its critics should use them to say what is happening in Arariboia.

Our assessment

This section expresses the site's position. It is separate from the facts set out above.

"The forest is burning, the Awá are hungry" may turn out to be true. Today it is more than is known, and taking it for granted weakens the question that matters. Brazil has one of the most serious systems in the world for protecting isolated peoples. For that very reason it can show that in Arariboia it worked: by publishing the group's health data, the state of invasions after 2025, the origin of this year's fires. A people that has chosen to speak with no one cannot tell us what happened to it. The state that must protect it can.

What to watch

  • Statements from Funai and Sesai on the group's health.
  • Whether the government publishes data on invasions of Arariboia and on the origin of the 2026 fires.
  • Whether the Federal Prosecutor's Office or Indigenous organisations bring the case back before the Supreme Court.

A note on the images

This piece does not publish photographs or footage of the Awá group. The images exist and are circulating, and we are not aware of any request from Funai not to distribute them: it is our own choice. A people in isolation has not consented to being filmed, and every image helps anyone who might want to locate them. Survival itself said it would have preferred the Awá to stay out of the spotlight. Non-contact, for us, applies to the gaze as well.
