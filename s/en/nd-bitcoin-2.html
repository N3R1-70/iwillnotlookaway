Bitcoin, who freezes and who flees: Russia's reserves, sanctions and the Hormuz toll

7 November 2026 — Moscow / Brussels / Strait of Hormuz

Series “Bitcoin, a refuge from whom?” · 2 of 4

In brief

  • In 2022 the West froze about 300 billion dollars of Russian reserves. The gold held in Moscow stayed untouched, and since then central banks have been buying gold as they had not for decades.
  • Those under sanctions use cryptocurrencies, but almost never Bitcoin: 84% of illicit volume goes through stablecoins pegged to the dollar. Illicit traffic remains below 1% of the total.
  • At Hormuz, Iran charges a toll payable in Bitcoin too, but large-scale payments cannot be seen. A toll on mere passage is prohibited by the law of the sea, whoever imposes it and whatever it asks in return.
  • Those who impose sanctions freeze stablecoins, which have an issuer. With Bitcoin they cannot: so they seize the keys.
  • The means is not the culprit: cartels have laundered hundreds of millions through large banks too.

On 28 February 2022, four days after the invasion of Ukraine, the European Union banned all transactions involving the reserves of the Russian central bank. The United States, the United Kingdom and the other allies did the same. Within days Moscow lost access to about 300 billion dollars, almost half its reserves: dollars, euros and government bonds held abroad. One part, however, stayed intact. The Bank of Russia's gold was in vaults in Moscow, and no one could touch it.

Every central bank in the world saw the scene. And each asked itself the same question: what if it happened to us?

In the first part we saw that Bitcoin has a property that neither gold in a foreign vault nor a bank account has: on the network no one can freeze it. Here we look at who really uses this property, who fights it, and how.

A premise: the means is not the culprit

Before talking about sanctions, weapons and tolls, one thing must be said clearly. Lawbreakers use whatever moves easily and is hard to seize, and they have always done so with everyone's money. Between 2006 and 2010 Mexican and Colombian cartels laundered at least 881 million dollars through HSBC's American branch, which in 2012 paid 1.9 billion to settle the case. In 2024 TD Bank pleaded guilty to money laundering for letting more than 670 million dollars from criminal networks pass through its accounts, and paid over 3 billion. No one concluded that the dollar was to blame.

States do, however, act on the tools that make crime easier. In 2019 the European Central Bank stopped issuing the 500-euro note because, it explained, it could "facilitate illicit activities": a million euros in those notes weighed little more than two kilos. The note remains legal tender, but no more are printed. The same reasoning is applied to cryptocurrencies today: the means is not put on trial; the aim is to make it less convenient for lawbreakers.

What follows, then, is not an indictment of Bitcoin. It is a map of who uses this property, who fights it and how.

1. States choose gold, not Bitcoin

Since 2022 central banks have been buying gold as they had not for decades. According to the European Central Bank, by the end of 2025 gold had become the largest item in world reserves, ahead of US Treasury bonds: this had not happened since the mid-1990s. Partly it is down to the price, which has risen enormously; partly to purchases, above a thousand tonnes a year for three years in a row.

Central banks have said why themselves. In a 2023 survey of 85 sovereign funds and central banks, the majority answered that the freezing of Russia's reserves had made gold more attractive. And more and more central banks are bringing it home: India, for example, repatriated more than a hundred tonnes from the Bank of England in 2024.

Bitcoin barely features in this race. Central banks buy an asset they have known for centuries, that can be touched and kept in a national vault. The lesson of Moscow, for them, is not "use something no one controls" but "keep your reserves where you control them".

There is a legal detail. Russia's reserves in Europe, today about 210 billion euros, almost all held by the Belgian company Euroclear, are immobilised, not confiscated: they still belong to Russia, but Moscow cannot use them. In December 2025 the Union decided to keep them frozen indefinitely, but it failed to use them to finance Ukraine, mainly because of Belgium's opposition, and borrowed 90 billion on the markets instead. The Russian central bank has sued the Union before the General Court in Luxembourg, and a Moscow court has ordered Euroclear to pay it compensation. How far one may go with a foreign central bank's assets is one of the most open questions in international law today.

2. Those under sanctions choose cryptocurrencies, but not Bitcoin

If states that fear sanctions choose gold, states already subject to them have chosen cryptocurrencies. According to the analytics firm Chainalysis, in 2025 cryptocurrency flows to sanctioned entities grew almost eightfold compared with the year before, driven above all by Russia, Iran and North Korea. It must be said straight away, though, that all illicit traffic remains below 1% of cryptocurrency transactions.

But the surprise is which cryptocurrencies. Not Bitcoin: 84% of illicit volume, Chainalysis writes, is in stablecoins, the digital coins pegged to the dollar. Bitcoin has fallen to about 7%; in 2020 it was the other way round. In short, those who want to escape the dollar system use digital dollars.

Russia tried to go further. In January 2025 a Russian company launched A7A5, a stablecoin pegged to the rouble and designed specifically to get around sanctions. In less than a year it moved more than 100 billion dollars, according to the analytics firm Elliptic. But it served mainly as a bridge to Tether, that is, back to the dollar. And after sanctions by the United States, the United Kingdom and the European Union its volume collapsed: the platforms that traded it were hit one by one.

North Korea takes another route: it steals. According to Chainalysis, in 2025 North Korean hackers stole more than 2 billion dollars in cryptocurrencies; the October 2026 theft from Binance, attributed by the FBI to North Korea, is worth three times as much on its own. According to Western governments, that money funds the missile and nuclear programmes. Pyongyang rejects the accusations.

3. Hormuz: the toll in cryptocurrencies

The newest case is that of the Strait of Hormuz, through which about a fifth of the world's oil passes. From mid-March 2026, with the war under way, the Revolutionary Guards began charging a toll on ships in transit: up to 2 million dollars per ship, payable in Chinese yuan, in Bitcoin or in stablecoins. On 30 and 31 March the Iranian parliament turned it into law, with the "Strait of Hormuz Management Plan". In May came a maritime "insurance" too, Hormuz Safe, with premiums in Bitcoin.

Headlines spoke of a "Bitcoin toll". The reality is more modest. The analytics firm TRM Labs wrote that it had found no evidence on the blockchain of large-scale payments, and according to several accounts Iran prefers stablecoins. Bitcoin, here, is above all a symbol: the promise of a payment system Washington cannot switch off.

In legal terms, the point is not what the toll is paid with, but whether it can be charged at all. The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea guarantees all ships "transit passage" through international straits, prohibits hampering or suspending it and provides for no toll on mere passage; even in territorial waters a coastal state may charge only for specific services rendered to a ship. Iran has signed the Convention but not ratified it, and argues that the right applies only to those who have ratified it. On this site we have already run the symmetry test: in July 2026 the United States too, which has not ratified the Convention but recognises its rules on transit, announced a 20% toll on the cargo of third-country ships (the news; the opinion). A toll on mere passage is contrary to the law of the sea whoever imposes it, and whatever it asks in return: yuan, Bitcoin, dollars, or eggs, chickens and timber. The means of payment does not make the toll more or less unlawful.

4. Who freezes: the new weapon is the stablecoin

If those fleeing sanctions use stablecoins, those imposing them have learned to use them in turn. Every stablecoin has an issuer, and the issuer can freeze the tokens in any wallet in the world. Tether, the most widely used, does so regularly at the request of the US authorities: in January 2026 it blocked in a single day about 182 million dollars in wallets linked to the Revolutionary Guards. After the Binance theft, Tether and Circle froze part of the stolen funds within hours. In the United States a July 2025 law, the GENIUS Act, requires stablecoin issuers to be able to freeze funds and to comply with sanctions.

The result is a paradox. Cryptocurrencies were supposed to free money from states; stablecoins have brought the dollar, and the power to freeze it, inside the blockchain.

5. And bitcoins? They take the keys

With Bitcoin, as we have seen, there is no one to order to freeze anything. But a state can do something else: take the keys.

In October 2025 the US Department of Justice launched the forfeiture of 127,271 bitcoins, the largest cryptocurrency seizure in its history. According to the prosecution they belonged to Chen Zhi, chairman of the Cambodian Prince group, accused of running online scams in compounds where trafficked workers were held by force. Chen Zhi and the group reject the accusations. In January 2026 Cambodia arrested him and handed him over to China, where he was formally arrested in July; in the United States the indictment is still pending, and the forfeiture case is still open in Brooklyn, where his lawyers have asked for it to be dismissed. Those bitcoins are already in government custody. If the forfeiture is upheld, they should not be sold: they would go into the Strategic Bitcoin Reserve created by Donald Trump in March 2025, which is fed exclusively with seized bitcoins and has so far bought none. Counting these too, the US government holds about 328,000 bitcoins: it is the largest public holder in the world.

So the asset that "no one can freeze" ends up, it too, in a state's safe. Not because the network allows it, but because people have keys, computers, homes and bank accounts, and states know where to look for them.

Our judgement: a refuge from whom?

The answer is twofold. Bitcoin protects from the power to freeze, and that power is neither good nor bad in itself. It is used by a European Union that blocks the reserves of a country that invaded its neighbour, and it is used by an authoritarian government that blocks the accounts of its opponents. The same property serves a family in flight and those who break the rules, exactly like cash, gold and an account at an accommodating bank. The blame, when there is any, lies with those who break the rules, not with the means they use.

On this, the law offers only one yardstick: whoever freezes, seizes or charges must do so under a law, before a judge, with the same criterion for everyone. It is the yardstick we use for Russia's reserves, for Chen Zhi's bitcoins, for the Hormuz tolls, whoever imposes them.
