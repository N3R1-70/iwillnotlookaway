Bitcoin in the mirror: how it works, compared with gold and with money

7 November 2026 — World

Series “Bitcoin, a refuge from whom?” · 1 of 4

In brief

  • Bitcoin is a public ledger of payments, copied on thousands of computers. No one issues it, and there will never be more than 21 million.
  • Whoever holds the keys owns the bitcoins. Those who leave them on a platform own a promise, like someone with gold in a vault or money in a bank.
  • On the network no one can stop a payment or freeze an account. Intervention is possible only at the entrances and exits, on the platforms.
  • In wartime none of the three is a perfect refuge: Bitcoin is the hardest to seize, gold the easiest to exchange, the money of a stable country the easiest to spend.
  • "Crypto" is not one thing. More than half the tokens created since 2021 are already dead; Bitcoin alone is worth about 59% of the market.

On 31 October 2008 the financial world was still buried under the rubble of Lehman Brothers, which had collapsed six weeks earlier. That day, on a mailing list for cryptography enthusiasts, someone signing as Satoshi Nakamoto published a nine-page paper: it described a "peer-to-peer electronic cash system" that needed no bank at all. On 3 January 2009 the network went live. In the first block of data Nakamoto left a message: that day's headline from the British newspaper The Times, "Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks". It was a signature and a manifesto. Who Nakamoto is, even today, no one knows.

Seventeen years later Bitcoin is called "digital gold", and some regard it as a safe haven. To find out whether it is one, we hold it up against the two benchmarks we all use: gold, the oldest refuge, and everyday money, the euro or the dollar. Economists call it fiat money, from the Latin for "let it be done": it has value because a state declares it legal tender, not because it is tied to anything physical. One feature at a time, without jargon.

Before we start: Bitcoin is not "crypto"

People often say "crypto" as if it were one thing. It is not. Since 2021 more than 20 million digital tokens have been created, and more than half have already stopped trading: in 2025 alone, according to the data platform CoinGecko, 11.6 million of them died. They were mostly memecoins, coins created as a joke or to speculate, often in a few minutes and with no project behind them: on Pump.fun, the site most used to launch them, about 98% never reach a real market.

Value, by contrast, is concentrated in very few names. On 7 November 2026 Bitcoin alone accounted for about 59% of the whole market for cryptocurrencies; Ethereum, the largest platform for automated contracts, about 11%; stablecoins pegged to the dollar another 11%. The entire memecoin sector is worth just over 1%, and more than a third of it is a single coin, Dogecoin, created in 2013 as a joke.

This series is about Bitcoin, and when it talks about something else, such as stablecoins, it says so. What holds for Bitcoin, the 21 million cap or a network no one controls, does not automatically hold for the others: every cryptocurrency has its own rules, and many have an issuer or a group of founders who can change them.

1. Who keeps the ledger

Bitcoin. The ledger of payments, called the blockchain, is public, and identical copies of it exist on thousands of computers around the world. Roughly every ten minutes a new "block" of payments is added, linked to the previous one so that no one can alter the past without everyone noticing. Anyone can read every transaction from 2009 to today.

Gold. A bar in a safe needs no ledger: it belongs to whoever holds it. But most investment gold sits in the vaults of banks and custodians, and there ownership is written in their own ledgers, which only they can see.

Money. Our account exists in the bank's ledgers, and accounts between banks in those of the central bank. They are private ledgers: the bank, the central bank and, when needed, the authorities can see them.

2. Who creates it

Bitcoin. No one. The rules, including the one on how many bitcoins can exist, are written into the software. Changing them would require the agreement of the great majority of those who run the network, and that is what makes them hard to touch.

Gold. Nature. Mines extract it, and no one decides how much will reach the market: it depends on how much is found and how much it costs to get out.

Money. The central bank creates it and, for the largest part, so do commercial banks every time they make a loan: the Bank of England itself explained this in 2014. It is a political and economic choice, and someone makes it.

3. How much of it exists

Bitcoin. Never more than 21 million. They are created as a reward for those who keep the network running, and roughly every four years that reward is halved: this is the so-called halving, and the latest was in April 2024. In March 2026 the twenty-millionth bitcoin was created: more than 95% of all those that will ever exist are already in circulation, and the last will arrive around 2140. Some are lost forever because their owners lost the keys: estimates range from two to four million.

Gold. It is scarce, but its total is not fixed. Throughout history about 220,000 tonnes have been mined, almost all still in existence, because gold does not get used up. Each year mines add less than 2%. If the price rises, it pays to dig more.

Money. There is no cap. The European Central Bank targets inflation of 2% a year: by design, money loses a little value every year. Sometimes much more: in October 2022 inflation in the euro area reached 10.6%, the highest level since the euro has existed.

4. How much energy

Bitcoin. Those who add blocks to the ledger are called miners. To do so they must solve a calculation that takes a lot of electricity, and whoever succeeds first receives the new bitcoins. The consumption is the price of security: to forge the ledger you would have to spend more energy than everyone else combined. According to the University of Cambridge, the network uses about half a percentage point of the world's electricity, as much as a medium-sized country.

Gold. Gold is "mined" too, literally: digging, crushing and refining rock uses a lot of energy. Estimates, however, vary widely. According to a 2021 analysis by Galaxy Digital, a firm that invests in Bitcoin, built on World Gold Council data, the gold industry then used about twice as much energy as Bitcoin; other estimates give figures close to the network's. On top of that comes the impact of mines on the land.

Money. Banks, branches, data centres and payment networks use energy too. A reliable, comparable estimate, however, does not exist: it is the least known term of the comparison.

5. Who really owns it

Bitcoin. A bitcoin is an entry in the public ledger, and only the holder of the corresponding "private key" can move it. Whoever has the key has the bitcoins; whoever loses it loses them. Many people, for convenience, leave their bitcoins on a trading platform, an exchange: in that case they own a promise from the exchange. Users have long repeated a motto that says it in five words: "Not your keys, not your coins". If the keys are not yours, neither are the bitcoins. On 29 October 2026 about 6.3 billion dollars in cryptocurrencies vanished from the wallets of Binance, the largest of these platforms, the biggest theft ever recorded; the FBI attributes it to North Korea. Binance set aside its own funds to cover customers' losses. This time it could: the guarantee, though, was the soundness of a private company.

Gold. The same rule applies. A bar at home is yours; gold in an account or a fund is a promise from whoever holds it. Venezuela knows this: about 31 tonnes of its reserves sit in the vaults of the Bank of England, and for years Caracas has been unable to get them back. London did not recognise Nicolás Maduro as the legitimate president, and a long court case followed over who could dispose of it. After Maduro's capture by the United States in January 2026, the British government said the gold would remain frozen; in October Venezuela's government and opposition formed a joint commission to ask for its return.

Money. A bank account is a promise too: the money is a claim on the bank. In the European Union that promise is guaranteed by law up to 100,000 euros per person per bank. Where the guarantee fails, the promise breaks: in Lebanon, since 2019, savers have been unable to withdraw their deposits, the pound has lost more than 90% of its value and the World Bank counts the crisis among the worst in the world since the mid-nineteenth century.

6. With or without a name

Bitcoin. Addresses do not carry the owner's name, but every transaction remains written in the public ledger forever. Bitcoin is pseudonymous, not anonymous: specialised firms follow the money from one address to another, and that is how within hours the funds stolen from Binance were linked to North Korean hackers. The name appears when the money passes through an exchange that asks for ID.

Gold. A bar has no name. But in many European countries, to buy gold above a certain threshold, the dealer must ask for ID.

Money. Cash is anonymous, a bank account is not. And cash will have a limit: from 10 July 2027, throughout the European Union, it will be forbidden to pay in cash more than 10,000 euros to a business.

7. Who can stop it

Bitcoin. On the network, no one. A confirmed payment cannot be reversed or stopped, not even if it is a theft, and it knows no borders or opening hours. For someone living under a regime that controls bank accounts, that is freedom; for someone who has to enforce a sanction, it is a problem. Intervention is possible only at the entrances and exits, that is, at the exchanges, which can freeze customers' accounts.

Gold. Physical gold can be seized: at home, in a safe, at the border. Gold deposited abroad can be blocked by whoever holds it, as the Venezuelan case shows.

Money. A transfer can be stopped, an account can be frozen, by a judge's order or a government sanction. It is the fastest tool states have.

A halfway house: stablecoins. There are digital coins that travel on a blockchain like Bitcoin but are pegged to a currency, almost always the dollar: one is worth one dollar. They are called stablecoins, and they are fiat money in digital dress. Like money, they have an issuer, a private company such as Tether or Circle, which can freeze them: after the Binance theft both did so within hours with part of the stolen funds. With bitcoins this is not possible. They come back in the second part.

8. In wartime

Imagine a family forced to leave its city under the bombs.

Money. Money in the bank may stay blocked, as in Lebanon; cash runs out, and in a country at war it can lose value fast.

Gold. It is valuable everywhere and depends on no bank. But it is heavy, it shows, and at the border or a checkpoint it can be seized.

Bitcoin. That family's bitcoins are not at home, nor in a computer, nor in a bank: they are written in the public ledger, which has copies in dozens of countries. If the city loses power, if the government shuts down the internet, if the phone is destroyed, the ledger keeps working elsewhere and loses nothing. It does not need restarting: it depends on no single country, and as long as part of the network stays on somewhere in the world, it keeps running. And the key can be carried in memory: the most widely used wallets turn it into twelve or twenty-four ordinary words, with which access can be rebuilt from any device. Wealth can cross a border inside a person's head, with no suitcases and no declarations. In March 2022, in the first days of the Russian invasion, the specialist press told the story of a Ukrainian refugee who reached Poland with about 2,000 dollars in bitcoin, almost half his savings, on a USB stick: he said he could also have written his words on a piece of paper and taken them with him.

But in wartime there are three risks, and they apply to all three. What changes is the degree.

Theft. Whoever has the safe's combination takes the gold; whoever has the account's credentials takes the euros; whoever has the twelve words takes the bitcoins. A threat is enough, at a checkpoint or at home. The difference lies in what happens next. With gold and with Bitcoin the theft is final: there is no one to ask to reverse it. With a bank account, at least in Europe, an unauthorised payment can be disputed, and the bank must refund it by the end of the following business day, unless it has reasonable grounds to suspect fraud by the customer: the EU Court of Justice confirmed this in October 2026. Meanwhile, physical attacks to force people to hand over their Bitcoin keys are rising sharply: in 2025 the security firm CertiK verified 72 of them worldwide, the highest number ever recorded.

Collapse in value. It applies to all three, at different speeds. Bitcoin can lose a lot in little time: on 28 February 2026, when the bombing of Iran began, it fell within minutes, while gold rose. Gold fluctuates less, but in 2026 it fell too. And money depends on which one: the euro has lost at most about a tenth of its purchasing power in a year; the Lebanese pound has lost more than 90%. For someone fleeing a country whose currency is collapsing, Bitcoin may be the lesser risk.

Who accepts it. Here too the problem is shared. A farmer does not want grains of gold in exchange for eggs, and abroad a shop may refuse euros. Bitcoins can be spent directly, without exchanging them: online, in some shops, between individuals, and with systems such as Lightning the payment arrives in a few seconds. But the difference lies in how easy they are to exchange. Euros and dollars can be exchanged at any bank in the world, gold at any jeweller; for bitcoins you need a connection and someone willing to take them. In 2021 El Salvador made them legal tender and required shops to accept them; in October 2024, according to a survey by Francisco Gavidia University, more than nine Salvadorans in ten said they did not use them to pay, and in January 2025, to obtain a loan from the International Monetary Fund, the country dropped the requirement.

In wartime, then, none of the three is a perfect refuge. Bitcoin is the hardest to seize and the easiest to take away; gold is the easiest to exchange anywhere; the money of a stable country is the easiest to spend and the only one that offers some protection against theft, while that of a country in crisis can be the worst of the three.

9. What it is worth, and why

Bitcoin. On its own it pays no interest or dividends and produces nothing: it is worth what someone is willing to pay for it. That is why its price moves so much: since its record of 126,000 dollars in October 2025 it has lost about 40%, and today it trades at around 76,000. Those who own it, however, can lend it to whomever they choose, as people do privately with gold or money. And services are emerging around Bitcoin that put it to work. With automated contracts on the blockchain, smart contracts, it can be pledged as collateral for a loan without selling it: since 2025, for example, a large American platform has allowed its customers to do this, requiring collateral at least a third larger than the amount lent. Almost always, though, to use these services the bitcoins must be handed to a custodian that turns them into a token usable on another network: the keys go back to an intermediary.

Gold. Gold pays no interest either. But it has real uses, in jewellery and industry, and buyers who do not sell in a panic: central banks, which in recent years have bought it at record rates. It moves less than Bitcoin, but it is not motionless: at the end of October 2026 both Bitcoin and gold were down about 7% since the start of the year, the last two in a ranking of 21 major asset classes.

Money. In the short run it is the most stable of the three: a euro today is worth a euro tomorrow. In the long run it loses purchasing power, a little each year by design and a lot when inflation runs high.

Bitcoin shares with gold its scarcity and the absence of an issuer. But gold can be touched, has existed for millennia and works without electricity; Bitcoin lives only as long as the internet and the power grid run, has seventeen years of history and is kept with a key that, if lost, cannot be recovered. With money it shares almost nothing: and that is precisely why some people choose it.

The question we started from remains. A safe haven protects from something. In 2026 Bitcoin did not protect from market crashes. But it has a property that neither gold in a foreign vault nor a bank account has: no one can freeze it. In the second part we look at who really uses this property, and against whom.
