In Ethiopia, the 2022 peace deal is cracking as drone strikes return to Tigray

July 19, 2026 — Ethiopia

“We fear it will further deteriorate” — Volker Türk, UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, February 10, 2026

The facts

In November 2022 the Pretoria Agreement ended one of the bloodiest wars of recent years: two years of conflict between the Ethiopian government and the Tigray People's Liberation Front, tens of thousands dead, more than two million displaced, of whom roughly one million remain uprooted today. In early 2026 that agreement began to crack — and it is not the only open front in the country.

On January 26, 2026, clashes between the Ethiopian National Defence Force (ENDF) and regional Tigray security forces intensified in the disputed Tselemti area along the Amhara border, after Tigrayan forces crossed the Tekeze river to retake it. In the following days Tigrayan forces occupied the disputed towns of Alamata and Korem in southern Tigray, while on January 30 the ENDF carried out drone strikes on vehicles in central Tigray, killing at least one person — local authorities said the vehicles were carrying civilian goods, cooking oil and bananas. On February 1 Tigrayan forces withdrew from Tselemti. UN High Commissioner for Human Rights Volker Türk said the situation remains volatile and risks further deterioration, citing the use of drones and artillery by both sides and mutual arrests over perceived affiliation with the enemy. The International Crisis Group, in an analysis published February 18, 2026, warns that the risk of the conflict widening to include Eritrea remains high, amid a build-up of proxy forces on both sides of the border, while the Tigray Front remains excluded from the June 1, 2026 federal elections for lack of an agreement on its registration as a party.

In parallel, since April 2023, the conflict between the federal government and the Fano militia in the Amhara region has continued without interruption. The Human Rights Watch 2026 report documents war crimes and other serious abuses by both sides, including drone strikes that harmed civilians, more than nine million children out of school due to conflict and natural disasters, a crackdown on civil society ahead of elections, and a transitional justice process that remains stalled. According to ACLED, more than 7,400 attacks were recorded nationwide between January 2022 and mid-May 2026: more than half (3,719) in Amhara alone, the hardest-hit region, with another 2,735 in Oromia, where the separatist conflict with the Oromo Liberation Army continues in parallel. Between March and May 2026 the rate of Fano attacks rose by more than 50% compared with the previous quarter, disrupting the organization of the vote in several areas right up to election day itself.

The government in Addis Ababa presents its operations as necessary to restore order against armed insurgents; the Tigray Front justifies the push on Tselemti as protecting Tigrayans who returned home but faced discrimination; Eritrea has not officially confirmed troop movements, and the accusations remain — for now — risk assessments by independent observers, not established facts.

Legal commentary

The Pretoria Agreement of November 2, 2022 (the Cessation of Hostilities Agreement) is the instrument whose erosion is at issue here. Under international humanitarian law, attacks on vehicles and areas with a civilian presence fall under Common Article 3 of the Geneva Conventions and Additional Protocol II, which prohibit indiscriminate attacks and require distinguishing between military and civilian objects in a non-international armed conflict. The characterization of some acts as possible war crimes, by both sides, comes from Human Rights Watch — an advocacy source with a declared position, not a neutral judgment.

Implications — symmetry test

HRW attributes war crimes and serious abuses to “both warring parties” — not only the government. The same standard must therefore apply both to ENDF drone strikes and to the actions of the TDF, Fano and the OLA: none of the four armed actors involved (the federal government, the Tigray Front, Fano, the OLA) should be judged by a different yardstick than the others.

A country with four armed fronts open simultaneously (Tigray, Amhara, Oromia, and the risk of Eritrean involvement) and a peace agreement crumbling three years after signature receives disproportionately little international coverage compared with Sudan or Gaza — exactly the gap between gravity and neglect this platform exists to close.
