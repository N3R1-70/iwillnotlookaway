Firing squads are back in Libya: in Benghazi, Haftar's military courts decide

25 October 2026 — Benghazi, Libya

In brief

  • Since 23 August the forces of General Khalifa Haftar, the de facto rulers of eastern Libya, have resumed carrying out death sentences. On 14 October the UN envoy told the Security Council that 58 people convicted of terrorism had been executed in Benghazi in two months.
  • Human rights organisations denounce trials before military courts, behind closed doors, based on confessions the detainees say they gave under torture. At least two men were reportedly shot after their sentence had been reduced to life imprisonment.
  • Libya had not carried out a death sentence since 2010. In the Gernada military prison at least fifty condemned men risk being next.
  • International law does not forbid Libya from having the death penalty. It forbids applying it after unfair trials and on the basis of extracted confessions.

The background

Libya has the death penalty in its code, but for fifteen years it did not apply it: a de facto moratorium, never written down, that survived the fall of Gaddafi and the civil war. Since 2014 the country has been split between the government in Tripoli, recognised by the UN, and the east controlled by Haftar and the House of Representatives in Benghazi (we covered this in October, in our analysis of the Tripoli agreement). Libya did not appear in our piece on the countries that still execute: now it does.

Many of the condemned had been arrested in Benghazi and Derna between 2014 and 2018, during the war between Haftar's forces and rival armed groups. Between 2018 and 2021 the military courts in the east tried hundreds of civilians (Human Rights Watch).

The facts

The first executions. On 23 August the eastern military prosecutor announced that ten people convicted of murder and terrorism had been shot. On 29 August six more were killed (Amnesty, ICJ, Libya Crimes Watch and OMCT). Two weeks earlier, on 10 August, a car bomb in Benghazi had killed Haftar's head of military intelligence, General Fawzi al-Mansouri (Al Jazeera). No source links the two.

How many. The counts keep rising and do not match. Local organisations had documented 31 by 10 September, 23 in Benghazi and 8 in Derna (JURIST). On 14 October the UN envoy Hanna Tetteh spoke of 58 executions in Benghazi over the previous two months (UNSMIL). In the Gernada military prison at least fifty men sentenced to death risk being next (Amnesty International).

How. According to Human Rights Watch and Amnesty, the trials took place before military courts, without notice and without the public: no families, no journalists. Some defendants could not choose their lawyer or see the evidence. The judgments carry no written reasons. Several of the condemned say they confessed under torture, after their arrest, to armed groups linked to Haftar. Families were not informed, bodies were not returned, funerals were banned. At least two men were shot even though a military appeals court had commuted their sentence to life imprisonment.

What they answer. The eastern authorities announced the executions by listing the charges, but we found no response from them to the criticism of the trials. On 14 October, before the Council, the UN envoy reported a first step: the east has agreed to open the death penalty case files to the mission and to allow visits to its prisons.

Who protested. On 16 September the UN mission called for executions to be suspended and procedures reviewed. The Tripoli government condemned them. On 30 September the European Union called for them to stop and for the trials to be reviewed by the UN mission (EEAS). In the Council, Denmark called for an official moratorium as a first step towards abolition (Denmark at the UN). The United States' statement at the same meeting, as published by its mission, speaks of the electoral agreement, drones and oil, but not of the executions (US Mission to the UN).

Legal commentary

The death penalty as such. Libya has not signed the protocol to the UN Covenant on Civil and Political Rights that abolishes the death penalty. International law therefore does not forbid it from having one. It forbids applying it like this.

The Covenant. Libya has been a party to the Covenant since 1970 (Human Rights Library, University of Minnesota). Article 6 allows capital punishment only for the most serious crimes and only after a final judgment by a competent court. Article 14 guarantees a fair and public trial. The UN Committee that interprets the Covenant is explicit: a death sentence handed down after an unfair trial violates the right to life (General Comment No. 36). The same Committee regards military trials of civilians as an exception to be justified case by case, not a rule (General Comment No. 32). And it regards keeping the date of execution and the place of burial secret as inhuman treatment of the family.

Torture. Libya ratified the UN Convention against Torture in 1989. A confession obtained by torture cannot be used as evidence in any proceedings (Article 15).

Libyan law. On 2 November 2025 the constitutional chamber of the Supreme Court in Tripoli declared unconstitutional the 2017 law that placed civilians accused of terrorism before military courts (Libya Herald). The east, however, disputes that chamber: the House of Representatives has created its own Constitutional Court in Benghazi, which has declared the Tripoli chamber abolished (Asharq Al-Awsat). The country's division reaches all the way to the question of which judge can say what is lawful. On one point, though, there is no need to choose a judge: a man whose sentence has been commuted no longer has a death sentence to carry out.

Who is responsible. The obligations under the Covenant belong to Libya. In the east they are exercised, in practice, by whoever controls the territory and the courts: Haftar's command.

Symmetry test

  • The east. The executions were decided and announced by its military institutions. The condemned were accused of serious crimes, some linked to a war in which the east suffered bombings and killings: the latest, that of al-Mansouri, in August. The seriousness of the charges, however, does not change the rules of the trial: it is precisely for the most serious crimes that the Covenant makes them stricter.
  • Tripoli. The UN-recognised government condemned the executions. But for the Mitiga prison in Tripoli, the International Criminal Court has confirmed charges of torture and killing of detainees against its former head, Khaled El Hishri, who will stand trial. The problem of detainees without guarantees is not the east's alone.
  • The outsiders. The European Union and Denmark spoke. The statement published by the United States, which through its envoy Massad Boulos negotiates directly with the Haftar family, did not. Human Rights Watch is asking the governments that back Haftar's forces to intervene: as of 25 October we found no public statement from them.
  • The yardstick. If a government hostile to the West shot dozens of opponents after secret military trials, would the reactions be louder? We cannot prove it. We can record that here, so far, they have been few.

What worked

The UN mission has obtained access to the death penalty case files and to the prisons. It is little, but it is the first door to open. The case reached the Security Council in the month in which it must renew the mission's mandate.

Our assessment

This section expresses the site's position. It is separate from the facts set out above.

Fifteen years without executions were not a law. They were a choice, repeated every day by those who could have signed an order and did not. That is why they can be broken in a single day.

We do not know why the east started again now. Perhaps to answer the Benghazi car bomb. Perhaps to show strength while talks go on about who will govern the country. We do not know. We know that the trials were secret, that the confessions are disputed and that at least two men died with a sentence that was no longer a death sentence.

"Every execution carried out under these conditions is an irreversible injustice," Human Rights Watch wrote. That is the word that matters: irreversible. A wrong trial can be held again. A firing squad cannot. Fifty men are still in Gernada. The Security Council, which is writing the mission's new mandate these days, has the chance to ask for one simple thing: that none of them be killed before someone has read their file.

What to watch

  • The renewal of the UN mission's mandate by 31 October: whether the text mentions the executions and the access to the files.
  • What the mission finds in the files and in the eastern prisons.
  • The fate of the Gernada detainees.
  • The General Assembly vote on a moratorium on the death penalty, at the end of the year, and how Libya will vote.
