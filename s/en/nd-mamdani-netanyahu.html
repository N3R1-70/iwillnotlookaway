A Mayor, an Arrest Warrant, and a Question No One Really Wants to Ask

July 20, 2026

"Netanyahu belongs in The Hague: he’s a war criminal charged by the International Criminal Court." — Zohran Mamdani, Mayor of New York, July 18, 2026

Not sure what the ICC is or how it works? A guide explains its jurisdiction, membership, and structural limits.

Read the explainer

The facts

On July 18, 2026, New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani confirmed, in an interview on The New York Times podcast "The Interview," that the city’s Law Department is actively reviewing whether and how to enforce the arrest warrant issued by the International Criminal Court (ICC) against Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, should he visit New York in September for the UN General Assembly. The warrant dates to November 2024, when the ICC issued it against Netanyahu and then-Defense Minister Yoav Gallant, finding reasonable grounds for crimes against humanity and war crimes in Gaza, including starvation as a method of warfare. The United States has never ratified the Rome Statute, the ICC’s founding treaty, and is therefore not legally bound to cooperate with its warrants — a point raised by US Ambassador to the UN Mike Waltz, who called Mamdani’s move "pure political theater," also citing head-of-state immunity and protections under the UN Headquarters Agreement for visiting foreign officials. Netanyahu’s office responded on July 19, calling the ICC a court with no jurisdiction over Americans or Israelis. Mamdani, for his part, said he intends to stay within existing legal bounds rather than write new laws to get around them.

On July 21, Mamdani made public the conclusion of the legal review in a video posted on X: the city lacks independent authority to enforce the warrant, while the federal government does — and he called on it to join the ICC and carry it out. He reiterated that Netanyahu is “not welcome” in New York, nor is any other fugitive war criminal. The day before, President Trump had publicly stated that Netanyahu would not be arrested “in any way, shape, or form” on US soil.

Israel’s UN Ambassador Danny Danon responded in a video accusing the mayor of previously seeking a meeting with Iran’s UN mission — blocked by the State Department — and of calling a Hamas sniper an innocent civilian; he argued that it is Mamdani, not Netanyahu, who should be arrested, and announced that the Israeli prime minister will speak from the General Assembly podium in September regardless.

Symmetry test

The principle at stake — whether a municipal authority can enforce an ICC warrant when its own state has not ratified the Rome Statute — does not depend on who the warrant targets. The same logic would apply if a mayor threatened to arrest an American official wanted under an ICC warrant, and this is not hypothetical: the Court has also investigated US personnel in Afghanistan, prompting US sanctions in response. The real issue is not the identity of the parties but the ICC’s own architecture: a tribunal whose authority rests on states’ voluntary membership, enforceable wherever there is political will to do so, and inert everywhere else. Mamdani’s appeal to the federal government does not change this picture either: Washington is no more obligated than New York to cooperate with the Court — the difference between the two is political, not legal. It is also worth noting, for the sake of method, that Danon’s response does not address the warrant’s merits or legal validity: it shifts the discussion to political and personal accusations, without touching the underlying structural problem.

Rhetorical escalation — update

On 23 July a Jewish man was stabbed outside a Jewish center in Manhattan. On 26 July, in a Fox News interview ("Sunday Morning Futures"), Netanyahu accused Mamdani of fomenting hate: "He’s fomenting hate. He’s supposed to be the mayor of all New Yorkers." — explicitly linking the stabbing to the mayor’s rhetoric on the ICC warrant, though none of the sources gathered establish any causal link found by an investigative authority. Netanyahu confirmed he still intends to travel to New York in September for the UN General Assembly, and called the ICC charges against him politically motivated. On 27 July Mamdani responded by declining to engage directly: "I’m not interested in getting into a back-and-forth with Prime Minister Netanyahu." — reiterating that his priority is the safety of every New Yorker, including Jewish New Yorkers, without retracting either the war-crimes accusation or his call for the federal government to enforce the warrant.

(The causal link Netanyahu suggests between Mamdani’s statement and the stabbing remains his own claim, not an independent finding in the sources gathered for this update — it should be reported as such, not as fact. A proper symmetry test would require a current, comparably verified case: the only precedent found in this session — an incitement accusation against Netanyahu himself, November 2016, over wildfires in Israel — is too dated and has no known outcome to support a direct comparison. We are not including it, so as not to build a weak symmetry purely for formal completeness.)
