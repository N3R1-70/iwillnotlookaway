Dozens dead in one cell: in Minna, Nigeria, the State is the accused

25 October 2026 — Minna, Nigeria

In brief

  • On 16 September Nigeria's paramilitary corps NSCDC arrested dozens of unlicensed gold miners near Minna, the capital of Niger State. On the night of the 17th, in the command's cell, 37 of them died according to the government; the mortuary received 33 bodies. According to the governor, many were between 14 and 18 years old.
  • The guard in charge of the cell admitted before the state commission of inquiry that he heard people screaming about the heat and the overcrowding, and did not open the door. The regional commander, suspended along with twenty-three other officers, speaks of "a very costly mistake". The official cause of death has not yet been established.
  • The federal government reacted quickly: suspensions, an inquiry, President Tinubu calling for prosecutions. But the federal panel's report, due on 21 October, has not been made public, and a human rights group is demanding its release.
  • Under international law a death in custody creates a presumption that the State is responsible, until a genuine investigation proves otherwise. Nigeria has had a law against torture since 2017, but so far it does not appear to have led to the conviction of any officer.
  • Stopping illegal mining is legitimate: in the north of the country lead from gold mining has killed hundreds of children. The way it was done in Minna is not.

The background

This is the site's second piece on Nigeria. The first, Nigeria: who is killed, who tells the story, who should prosecute, described the violence of armed groups and the clash of narratives about the dead. It also explained that part of that violence is sustained by gold mined in secret in the north of the country. This piece looks at the same economy from the opposite side: what happens when the State tries to stop it.

In Nigeria thousands of people dig for gold without a licence, by hand, in pits and tunnels. Many are teenagers. The work is dangerous even for those who live next to the mines: since 2010, in Zamfara State, lead released by gold processing has killed more than 400 children (Human Rights Watch). In 2024 the government created a dedicated unit against illegal mining, the "Mining Marshals", placed under the civil defence corps NSCDC (Voice of Nigeria).

The NSCDC is an armed paramilitary corps under the Ministry of the Interior. It was created to protect infrastructure and public property, but it has powers of arrest and its own cells.

The facts

The raid. Residents of a housing estate on the outskirts of Minna, M.I. Wushishi, had long complained about miners digging close to their homes. On 15 September the NSCDC commander for Niger State, Suberu Aniviye, visited the site; on the evening of the 16th his men arrested dozens of people between Wushishi and Lukoto (TheCable). Who carried out the raid is already disputed. The commander says he sent the Mining Marshals. Their national commander denies it: the unit answers only to headquarters in Abuja, none of its men were in Minna, and he learned of the operation from the newspapers (Naija News). The figures on the number of detainees do not match either: 67 according to the governor, Umaru Bago (Sahara Reporters), 58 according to the cell guard, including 25 women, in a facility meant for 28 to 30 people (THEWILL).

The night in the cell. On 17 September, at the NSCDC command in Minna, dozens of detainees were found dead. According to the governor many were between 14 and 18 years old and worked in the mines instead of going to school (Reuters). Survivors interviewed by the Nigerian daily Premium Times describe a small, windowless room with up to 65 people inside. They say that towards evening they began banging on the door asking for water, that they lifted the youngest up towards a vent so they could breathe, and that when the door was opened many were already dead (Premium Times). Among the survivors is a nine-year-old boy (Sahara Reporters). One mother told Reuters that officers were asking for 100,000 naira, about 75 dollars, to release the detainees.

What the guard says. Before the state commission of inquiry, chaired by Justice Mohammed Mohammed, the officer in charge of the cell, Usman Isah Ndamaka, confirmed the core of those accounts. He said he heard the detainees screaming about the heat and the crush, and did not open because he was alone and feared being overpowered. He apologised in tears: "I admit my mistake" (THEWILL). Still to be verified is the claim by some survivors that in the days before, officers had sprayed a substance in the cell presented as mosquito repellent.

How many died. The state government and almost all media report 37 dead. Before the state commission, however, the head of the Minna hospital mortuary said he received 33 bodies, brought in two ambulance trips, and could not account for bodies that never arrived (Leadership via allAfrica). The list of 37 names, released by a human rights association, started from the 33 registered at the hospital; four more names were added after families reported missing relatives (TheNigeriaLawyer). Where those four bodies are, and how they died, no one has yet explained in public.

The cause of death. It has not been established. The NSCDC first spoke of a possible disease outbreak. The governor did not rule out exposure to toxic substances from the mines. According to an intelligence report cited by AFP, overcrowding and lack of air may have contributed (CrispNG). The victims were buried quickly, according to Islamic tradition. Toxicology tests require the families' consent and the samples are going to a laboratory in Lagos; so far the commission has not received conclusive forensic evidence (THEWILL).

The anger. The next day hundreds of people took to the streets in Minna, some with sticks, machetes and hunting rifles; some tried to set fire to public buildings. Security forces fired live rounds (Reuters). A 24-year-old told AFP he was shot while returning from the funeral of one of the miners (Africanews). A curfew was imposed.

The State's response. It was quick. The government suspended the commander and some twenty officers, later rising to 24: those who made the arrests, those who investigated, the cell guards and the legal office. President Bola Tinubu ordered an inquiry and said that suspensions are no substitute for justice: those responsible must be prosecuted. The Minister of the Interior appointed a ten-member panel, including a doctor, a human rights activist and a representative of the miners. Niger State opened its own commission, chaired by a judge. The vice-president announced 250 million naira for the families.

What has emerged so far. On 6 October the chairman of the federal panel, Jonathan Kure, spoke of "elements of institutional breakdown" and "overzealousness", and asked for two more weeks, until 21 October, pending the test results (Vanguard). On 17 October, before the state commission, the suspended commander apologised to the families: "A mistake was made, but a very costly one". He said the detainees were due to appear before a judge precisely on the 17th. The head of his legal office said he had advised him to release the minors to their parents, and did not know why that was not done (Channels TV). 21 October passed without the federal report being made public. On the 22nd a Nigerian diaspora human rights group, NDOCHR, called for its immediate release and announced a protest outside NSCDC headquarters in Abuja (The Guardian Nigeria).

Legal commentary

Whoever dies in custody dies in the hands of the State. The International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, which Nigeria has ratified, protects the right to life. The UN Committee that monitors it puts it plainly: a death in custody in unnatural circumstances creates a presumption that the State has arbitrarily deprived someone of life, and the presumption falls only if a proper investigation proves otherwise (General Comment No. 36, para. 29). It is not for the families to prove the officers' guilt: it is for the State to prove it did everything to keep alive those it had locked up. Whether the cause was asphyxia, a substance or a disease, those shut in that cell depended on the State for air and water. The guard who hears the screams and does not open is the textbook case: the duty to protect does not depend on how many officers are on duty that night.

The teenagers. The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, ratified by Nigeria, allows the detention of a minor only as a last resort and for the shortest possible time, and requires that children be separated from adults (art. 37). In Minna, according to the accounts, 14-year-olds were in the same cell as adults, and the advice to release them to their parents was not followed. That they worked in the mines instead of going to school is not their fault: it is one more thing the State should have prevented.

Before a judge within a day. The Nigerian Constitution requires that anyone arrested be brought before a judge within one day, if there is a court within 40 kilometres (s. 35). The commander says the hearing was set for the 17th, the day after the arrest. Some survivors speak instead of three days in detention. It is one of the points the commissions must clarify.

Torture. Nigeria ratified the UN Convention against Torture in 2001 and its Optional Protocol, which provides for inspections of places of detention, in 2009. Since 2017 it has had a national law against torture: it provides for up to 25 years in prison and, if torture causes death, a murder charge (UNCAT). Denying water and air to people locked in an overcrowded room can amount to cruel and inhuman treatment, even without intent to kill. But the law has a difficult precedent: in 2020 Amnesty International documented 82 cases of torture and killings by the SARS police unit, disbanded the same year after mass protests, and no officer had been prosecuted (Amnesty International).

Who investigates. The UN rules for investigating suspicious deaths, the so-called Minnesota Protocol, require that the investigation be independent of the body involved, prompt, transparent and open to the families. The federal panel was appointed by the Ministry of the Interior, the same ministry the NSCDC answers to, and so far it has published nothing. The public hearings of the state commission, chaired by a judge, are instead where the admissions have come from.

Symmetry test

  • Illegal mining is a real problem. Minna residents had asked for the digging near their homes to be stopped, and gold mined in secret also finances the armed gangs of the north-west. Lead from the mines has killed hundreds of children. The State has the right, and the duty, to intervene. The problem is not the raid, it is what happened afterwards.
  • The protest was not peaceful. Some protesters were armed and tried to set fire to public buildings. This does not justify firing live rounds into the crowd, but it explains why a curfew was imposed.
  • The government did more than usual. Within 24 hours came the suspensions, the president called for prosecutions, the commander and the guard admitted their mistakes in public. In the SARS cases none of this happened. It remains to be seen whether words turn into trials.
  • It does not happen only in Nigeria. In Italy Stefano Cucchi died in 2009 a week after his arrest. It took more than twelve years and several trials, including for the cover-ups, before the Court of Cassation convicted two Carabinieri of manslaughter (Il Fatto Quotidiano). Deaths in custody happen in democracies too. What makes the difference is what happens next.

Our assessment

This section expresses the site's position. It is separate from the facts set out above.

In Minna there was no war, no jihadist attack, no bandit raid. There were teenagers digging for gold and a State that wanted to stop them. They died in a room locked by those who were supposed to bring them before a judge, while the man holding the key heard the screams. That is why this case says more about Nigeria than many massacres: it measures not the strength of armed groups, but the State's ability to respect the rules it asks others to respect.

The government's reaction deserves credit: immediate suspensions, a president calling for prosecutions, officers admitting their mistake in public. That is more than was seen after the SARS cases. But the first deadline has already passed: the federal report was due on 21 October and is not public. Nigerian commissions of inquiry have a long history of reports delivered and never published. The test remains simple: whether the report is published in full, whether anyone is tried under the anti-torture law or for murder, and whether the families receive compensation decided by a judge and not just a donation.

In our first piece on Nigeria we wrote that violence in the country is told above all through the religion of the victims. These deaths fit neither narrative: they were not killed by jihadists or armed herders. They died in a prison, in the hands of State officers. In Europe they have barely been mentioned. That too is a way of looking away.

What to watch

  • Whether and when the federal panel's report is published, and whether in full.
  • The results of the autopsies and toxicology tests.
  • The conclusions of the Niger State judicial commission: who ordered the raid, how many detainees there were, the gap between 37 and 33 dead.
  • Whether the suspended officers are charged, and with what offence.
  • Compensation for the families, and new rules for NSCDC cells.
