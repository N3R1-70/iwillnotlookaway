ANALYSIS

The UN says it isn’t safe to return to Syria. Turkey and Germany are sending people back anyway.

26 July 2026 — Syria / Turkey / Germany

Syria in 2026 is receiving two opposite flows at once. Governments hosting Syrian refugees — from Turkey to Germany — are pushing for return, convinced that Assad’s fall in December 2024 closed the most acute phase of the crisis. But when the United States and Israel opened a war front against Iran in late February 2026 that drew in Lebanon too, more than 65,000 people fled into Syria — the very country held up as a safe destination is receiving new displacement. Meanwhile, 15.6 million Syrians out of roughly 23 million residents need humanitarian assistance, and winter storms in December 2025-January 2026 destroyed hundreds of shelters in the north of the country.

The UNHCR report

On 28 May 2026 the UN refugee agency published a formal legal assessment (HCR/PC/SYR/2026/1) ruling out the possibility of safe return to Syria, calling non-refoulement a peremptory norm (jus cogens) — binding even for those who haven’t yet received a decision on their asylum claim.

Turkey

It hosts more Syrian refugees than any other country: over 3.6 million people. It also has the harshest, best-documented return pattern. Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International have documented cases of people arrested in their homes, beaten in detention centres, forced to sign “voluntary return” forms they weren’t allowed to read, and driven to the border in handcuffs — one Turkish official was quoted telling a deportee: “We’ll shoot anyone who tries to cross back.” Turkish authorities maintain that over 315,000 people have returned entirely voluntarily. A May 2026 report based on January-March data confirms the pressure is far from over: since January 2026 refugees have lost automatic health coverage, legal protection space is narrowing, and financial hardship remains the top stated reason for “choosing” to return.

Germany

On 30 March 2026 Chancellor Friedrich Merz hosted Syria’s interim president Ahmed al-Sharaa in Berlin, declaring the shared goal of returning 80% of Syrians in Germany within three years. The government resumed deportations, reaching a fourth case in late January 2026 — while Germany’s own foreign minister, after visiting Syria, had publicly voiced skepticism about a dignified return. Merz’s remarks triggered domestic backlash too: economists and politicians from several parties called the target unrealistic, warning it could worsen labour shortages in some sectors.

A precedent, not a third case

Worth a note, not a third equivalent example: Denmark had already opened this path in 2021, revoking residence permits by declaring the Damascus area safe — then under the Assad regime, a move sharply criticised by UNHCR and Amnesty International. After Assad’s fall, Denmark’s Refugee Appeals Board suspended Syrian cases, resuming only in June 2025: by March 2026 it had reviewed just ten, a cautious, case-by-case approach, unlike Turkey and Germany’s active push.

The symmetry test

Two radically different states — Turkey, outside the EU and an accession candidate for two decades, and Germany, the Union’s engine — apply the same selective standard to the same legal obligation: accept it when convenient, bypass it when domestic political pressure rises. The standard doesn’t change when you swap the actors: only the form of the violation changes, administrative in Berlin, physical in Ankara — not its legal substance. This doesn’t equate the severity of the two cases with each other, nor with the armed conflicts this site covers elsewhere. It’s a separate judgment, stated as such: the gap between what international refugee law requires and what signatory states do under electoral pressure doesn’t need an armed conflict to be documented — and its scale, in Turkey, remains the larger of the two.
