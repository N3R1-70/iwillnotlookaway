OPINION

Abstention as Diagnosis, Not Prescription

July 31, 2026

The vote is not a market: it is the only good whose absence does not lower the price — it gives it away. — N3R1-70

The right to vote is, historically, one of the most costly achievements of humanity: generations of people died to obtain it, or to extend it to those excluded from it. I start from this, and never forget it in what follows. But precisely because the vote is sacred, I believe it is worth asking honestly what happens when the formal act of voting stops producing the effect it was designed for: translating the will of voters into decisions that change something in people's real lives.

Article 21 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights establishes that everyone has the right to take part in the government of their country, directly or through freely chosen representatives, and that the will of the people, expressed through periodic and genuine elections, is the basis of governmental authority. The weak point of this architecture has never been the principle, but its practical hold: what happens when elections remain periodic and formally genuine, but the link between electoral promise and governing behaviour thins almost to the point of disappearing?

This is where the abstentionist argument enters, and I think it deserves to be taken seriously rather than dismissed as renunciation or disengagement. If public debate — mediated by a social ecosystem that rewards conflict and simplification more than proposals, and by a television journalism structured around confrontation rather than analysis — systematically reduces every electoral platform to interchangeable slogans between political forces, then the voter is no longer choosing between different visions for the country: they are choosing between communication packages optimised for the short term. In this scenario, the vote is not technically less free, but it is less informative: it no longer transmits to the political system a distinguishable signal of what the electorate actually wants, because no option on the table has genuinely articulated a long-term vision that can be checked against results over time.

Those who choose abstention as a response to this condition are not, in their own intention, giving up the right to vote: they are signalling that the political offer on the table does not, on its merits, deserve the legitimacy that a vote confers. It is a form of dissent internal to the democratic system itself, not against it.

That said, I believe abstentionism has a limit that anyone proposing it has a duty to face, not sidestep. A vote is not a market in which a missing buyer leaves the price unchanged: it is a near-zero-sum mechanism in which someone who does not show up takes power away from no one — they simply leave it entirely in the hands of whoever organises and turns out anyway, whether structured parties, cohesive interest groups, or more ideologically compact electorates. The recent history of many democracies shows that falling turnout tends to reward those who already have organisational machinery and voting discipline, not those who wish to punish the whole system through their own absence. In this sense, abstention risks being a signal that the political system is structurally equipped not to receive: a party that wins with halved turnout does not necessarily read that halving as a rejection of itself, but as open ground.

There is also a dimension that makes the phenomenon more structural than a simple communication defect. In an interconnected global economy, a large share of the variables that determine people's real wellbeing — capital flows, monetary policy, supply chains, energy prices — is decided in venues that largely escape the control of any single elected government, however broad its mandate. Electoral politics thus ends up operating in a residual space, often too narrow to keep the promises made during a campaign, because those promises were built to capture immediate consent rather than to reckon with the real constraints within which whoever governs will then have to move. When a programme fails or goes unfulfilled, the accountability mechanism that should follow — electoral sanction, the end of a political career — systematically jams: blame is pinned on external constraints, on allies, on opponents, almost never on a failure of realism in the original promise; and whoever failed is often found back on the candidate list, heading it, or reassigned to another post, rather than removed from public life. I believe this is a critique of the capture of politics by interests and constraints that remain outside direct democratic control, and of the lack of real consequences for those who promise things they know, or should know, they cannot deliver — not a claim of equivalence between democratic and autocratic systems, which remain distinct on an essential level: genuine alternation, the legal removability of those in power, the pluralism of actors competing for consent. It is precisely because that distinction remains true and worth defending that its silent erosion — not in form, but in the substance of accountability — deserves to be named with this degree of alarm.

Added to this, in these very months, is the European debate over rearmament and dependence on weapons systems and digital infrastructure supplied by foreign powers. The concern — raised publicly even by figures such as Wolfgang Ischinger, former head of the Munich Security Conference, regarding Germany's F-35s — is that software and logistical control over these systems remains in the hands of the producing country even after sale, with real dependency margins that go beyond a simple purchase of goods. It should be said with equal honesty, however, that the idea of an actual "switch" able to disable an aircraft remotely mid-flight has been explicitly denied both by the US Department of Defense and by Lockheed Martin, the manufacturer, and remains — as far as public verification currently shows — an unproven claim, even if fed by a real margin of opacity around remote update and maintenance systems that stay under the manufacturer's control. The legitimate doubt, then, concerns the degree of technological dependence and operational sovereignty a country gives up when it entrusts its defence to systems built, updated and maintained by another state — not a verified technical certainty, and it is shown here explicitly as such.

In the same period, according to the Oxfam report "Resisting the Rule of the Rich", presented in Davos in January 2026, the world's billionaires' collective wealth rose by $2.5 trillion in 2025 — a figure nearly equal to the total wealth held by the poorer half of humanity, roughly 4.1 billion people; in the same period, one in four people worldwide did not have regular access to enough food. The same report finds that billionaires are today more than 4,000 times more likely to hold political office than an ordinary person. This is not a subjective impression: it is a measured gap, published with a stated methodology, widening precisely as electoral politics — for the reasons discussed above — appears less and less able to intervene on the variables that determine it.

But here I think it must be said clearly, as a point the reasoning followed so far makes almost unavoidable: if abstention is the correct diagnosis of a structural illness — the draining of substance from political debate, capture by constraints that remain outside direct democratic control, the collapse of the accountability mechanism — then abstention itself cannot also be the cure. Abstaining signals that something is not working; it rebuilds nothing of what is not working. If anything, given the mechanism described above — falling turnout that benefits whoever mobilises regardless — abstention risks aggravating exactly the symptom it means to denounce, leaving open ground precisely to those least exposed to pressure for change.

The cure, if one exists, cannot lie in election day itself: it lies in rebuilding sites of political pressure that stay active between one election and the next, capable of forcing the electoral offer to reckon with reality instead of the short cycle of media consensus — intermediate bodies, organised mobilisation on specific issues, ongoing public monitoring of promises against facts, direct participation in places (often local) where the link between what is promised and what happens remains checkable. Critical voting, a blank or spoiled ballot counted and published, remain legitimate signals on election day itself — but they are signals, not substitutes for the organised pressure that should follow them in the months after, the kind that has historically produced real change when the vote alone was not enough.

I believe, though, that presenting this path as a "cure" without facing its real cost head-on would be another form of dishonesty. The same dynamics described above — an information ecosystem that rewards confrontation over proposals, an unbroken succession of controversies that fizzle out without ever reaching a visible resolution — do not leave voters merely uninformed: they leave them tired, in a way that makes organised participation harder, not easier. Anyone who tries to engage beyond the ballot, outside already institutionalised channels, often meets a second discouragement: being seen as odd, "strange", out of place — the exact opposite of what it should be, namely the normal condition of an active citizenry. Whether this outcome is the product of deliberate design or, more simply, the unplanned consequence of an incentive system that rewards whoever generates conflict and short-term attention regardless, cannot be demonstrated here with the same evidence this site requires elsewhere — and I do not claim it as such. But the practical effect is identical either way: a tired, discouraged population that struggles to picture as workable the very path this piece names as the only real cure. Acknowledging this is not an argument against trying to rebuild organised political pressure — it is the realistic premise without which that attempt would remain, I think, an abstract piece of advice, sound on paper and nearly impossible for the reader to put into practice.

There are, in any case, alternatives that carry the same signal of dissent without ceding the field: critical votes for smaller or new formations, blank or spoiled ballots where the electoral system counts and publishes them separately from turnout (making dissent visible instead of hiding it inside abstention), or forms of political participation that live outside election day alone — from organised pressure between one election and the next to direct participation at the local level, where the link between promise and reality stays more checkable.

It is not this site's job to tell readers what to do on election day. But if public debate has been reduced to click-bait — from social media to television — if the constraints that really matter are decided elsewhere, and if the gap between those with power to set the rules and those who bear them widens measurably, then the useful question is not "vote or abstain", but where and how, between one election and the next, the collective capacity to exert real pressure on those who promise and then fail to answer gets rebuilt — knowing, without illusions, that this is the harder path, not the more comfortable one.
