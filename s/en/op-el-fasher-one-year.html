The Earthen Wall

Editorial by N3R1-70

There is a wall around El Fasher. It is not made of concrete: it is earth piled up by bulldozers, an embankment more than fifty kilometres long. The Rapid Support Forces (RSF) raised it during the siege to close off the city: no food and no medicine in, no one out (AFP, via Kuwait Times). Two months before the city fell, on 26 October 2025, UNICEF estimated that 260,000 civilians were still behind that wall, half of them children (UNICEF, 26/8/2025).

A year later, the wall is still there. In September witnesses said the RSF were still reinforcing it. In October people who went back to see their homes were arrested and accused of spying for the army; a source close to the RSF confirms the arrests and calls them security measures (Darfur24, 6/10/2026).

And here is the point. That wall could be seen from space. Yale University's Humanitarian Research Lab followed it on satellite images as it grew (allAfrica, 1/9/2025), and its director, Nathaniel Raymond, later described what followed to the British Parliament as a collective failure to prevent the massacre (written evidence, 23/6/2026).

Behind the wall

In the 48 hours after the fall, according to monitoring groups, at least 1,500 people were killed. The same Yale lab documented house-to-house killings and, in the weeks that followed, a systematic campaign to make the bodies disappear: buried, burned, removed (Al Jazeera, 16/12/2025). In February the UN fact-finding mission concluded that in El Fasher the RSF committed at least three of the acts listed in the Genocide Convention against the Zaghawa and the Fur (UN Geneva). It is the finding of a commission, not a judgment. The RSF deny large-scale killings.

Those who managed to get out reached Tawila, sixty kilometres away, often on foot, after days of walking (MSF). Many did not make it: far fewer people reached Tawila than had been in the city (Al Jazeera, 3/11/2025). There is no way of telling this that makes it lighter, and I am not looking for one.

The Sudanese government objects to putting the army on the same level as a militia accused of such grave crimes (SUNA, via allAfrica). It is a serious argument: El Fasher has no equivalent on the other side. But this war does not have only one culprit. In the first three weeks of October drones killed at least 306 civilians in Sudan, and the UN human rights office attributes the strikes to both sides: the RSF and the army (UN News, 23/10/2026). On 19 October a drone hit the Amiet market, in Abyei, killing more than eighty people according to the UN. South Sudan blames the Sudanese army; a Sudanese officer, speaking anonymously, said the target was fuel tankers heading to the RSF (Al Jazeera, 21/10/2026). On 23 October army drones struck El Fasher, the market included (Darfur24, 23/10/2026). A market is still a market, whoever hits it.

On 7 October the army chief, Abdel Fattah al-Burhan, rejected negotiations and promised to retake "every inch" of the territory held by the RSF (Al Jazeera). Two weeks later, in Geneva, representatives of 27 countries repeated that there is no military solution (The National, 23/10/2026).

What I think

What follows is my assessment, not a fact.

The day after El Fasher fell, Trump's envoy for Africa, Massad Boulos, said the world was watching with great concern (allAfrica). He was right. The world was watching. We watched the wall grow for months, in public images. The problem was never seeing. It is what you do afterwards.

Afterwards came the reports, the condemnations, the one-year renewal of the fact-finding mission, with 28 votes in favour, 10 against and 9 abstentions (allAfrica, 6/10/2026). Then the sanctions by the United States, the European Union and the United Kingdom against individual RSF commanders (Arab News, 19/2/2026). Then the "breakthrough" announced in July by the prosecutor's office of the International Criminal Court, which says it can link the crimes to the leadership (Reuters, via NBC). Then the Geneva meetings. No arrest warrant made public for the crimes of this war (Middle East Eye). And the UN humanitarian plan for Sudan, in early October, was less than half funded (Sudan Tribune, 13/10/2026).

Why? Perhaps because Sudan is far away. Perhaps because the arms suppliers of both sides are countries nobody wants to quarrel with. Perhaps because a massacre without cameras makes less news. I do not know for certain: we looked for part of the answer by comparing what Europe does for Ukraine with what it does for Sudan (Why Ukraine and not Sudan). I know that the wall around El Fasher could be seen, and that seeing it stopped no one.

And one siege teaches the next. In July the mission warned that around El Obeid, a city of half a million people, the RSF were using the tactics of El Fasher (Al Jazeera, 9/7/2026). When a siege costs little to those who carry it out, it becomes a method. The sanctions hit assets those commanders are unlikely to hold in the West (Sudan War Monitor). There have been no arrests.

There is a part that concerns us too. According to the mission there are "reasonable grounds" to believe that the United Arab Emirates are a hub of the network supplying the RSF; the Emirates deny any role. And the Emirates also buy weapons from Italy. It is not proven that Italian weapons have reached Sudan, and I do not write that. I write that the law requires those who sell weapons to assess the risk that they end up being used against civilians, and that after El Fasher nobody can claim not to know that risk (Sudan, war by remote).

The wall of El Fasher is still there. It can be seen from above, as it could a year ago. The world is watching with great concern.

I will not look away. But watching, on its own, has never saved anyone.
