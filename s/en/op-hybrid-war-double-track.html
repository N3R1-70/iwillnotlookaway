Hybrid war, hidden evidence: escalation on two tracks

September 2, 2026

"We are not at war, but we are a daily target of hybrid attacks." — Alexander Dobrindt, German Interior Minister, September 1, 2026

On the night of August 4-5, 2026, a drone loaded with explosives was found near a Ukrainian Antonov cargo plane at Leipzig/Halle airport — a hub that has also served as a logistics link for military aid to Kyiv since 2022. From there began a chain of events that, a month later, escalated into a formal state-to-state accusation. I followed the facts in the order they happened, and tried at every step to keep what is verified separate from what is merely plausible.

What happened

The device failed to explode due to a detonator malfunction. A second, unidentified aircraft struck a DHL cargo plane at 400 meters altitude, six kilometers from the airport, forcing it to abort landing: the pilot diverted to Hanover, where the plane sustained minor damage to its nose. Ten days later, a third drone with additional military-grade explosive was found in the same area. On September 1, the German government formally attributed the attack to Russia: closure of the Bonn consulate and the Berlin Russian House, sanctions under discussion with the EU, full solidarity declared by NATO. Moscow rejects all of it as fabricated evidence.

An alternative hypothesis on intent — attributed, not assumed

One doctrinal reading of hybrid warfare holds that a drone doesn't need to explode to succeed: the goal of such incursions would be to test European air-defense reaction times, map radar coverage gaps, and demonstrate the permeability of critical infrastructure without opening direct conflict. This reading is consistent with "gray zone" doctrine, held by those who see these episodes as systematic tests rather than failed attacks — but it remains a doctrinal interpretation, not a fact established by any official source on the specific Leipzig case. I keep it separate from the factual account for the same reason I keep the false-flag hypothesis below separate: both are plausible, neither is proven.

The attribution problem

The material fact is not in dispute; how much of the intelligence behind the attribution is public, is. Germany is not the first to link Moscow to drone incursions in Europe, and the number of incidents attributed to Russian hybrid operations on the continent has well exceeded a hundred since the start of the year — that alone doesn't make the accusation an invention. But today neither version has complete public evidence: not the attribution to Moscow, nor the false-flag hypothesis raised by the Kremlin, which in turn offers no verifiable element in support. "It's convenient for someone" is not proof, in either direction.

Diplomacy exists

Since June 17, 2026, European Council President Antonio Costa has maintained a direct diplomatic channel with the Kremlin — contacts described by EU sources themselves as "brief, without substantive exchange," opened to be ready for negotiation once conditions allow. It isn't true that everything is pressure and nothing is dialogue: the two tracks run in parallel, and it remains fair to ask whether the second receives the same political energy as the first — but it is not accurate to write that diplomacy isn't working at all.

Who profits — with numbers, not impressions

In 2026, Italy's defense spending reaches €32.4 billion, a historic record. The ReArm Europe plan aims to generate up to €650 billion in additional fiscal space over four years. In the same period, lobbying spending in Brussels by Europe's ten largest arms manufacturers grew by 40% in one year, with 197 meetings between MEPs and the defense industry — more than double the previous five-year average. One necessary correction, though: the Volkswagen crisis, with up to 50,000 jobs at risk worldwide and 37,000 departures already agreed, is not — by CEO Blume's own admission — the flip side of a conversion to "military capitalism": the stated cause is Chinese competition, US tariffs, production overcapacity, and labor costs. Rearmament and the automotive crisis remain two parallel dynamics of German capitalism, not cause and effect — linking them would be the same causal shortcut I reject when I see it applied elsewhere.

A second front, only sketched here

In December 2025, the EU made permanent the freeze of roughly €200 billion in Russian Central Bank assets, eliminating the six-monthly renewal vote — a move Russia is legally contesting, with a $226 billion damages claim against the Belgian depositary Euroclear, and which raises a far from marginal question of international law: sovereign immunity, and what happens when a state reserve parked abroad stops being, in practice, untouchable. I won't develop this further here: it deserves its own dossier, with a dedicated symmetry test — what happens when another state freezes Western assets? — and sources verified one by one. I flag it here only as a trigger for a future thread.

The judgment

There is no certain proof that the Leipzig drone was invented to justify rearmament — I have no proof of it, and those who claim it have no more solid proof than those who claim the opposite. But I believe the "cui prodest" question has a partial, verifiable answer in public budgets: whether the episode is real, fake, or something in between, the flow of money toward the defense industry is already running, growing, and finding in the escalation — real or perceived — its most convenient justification. Keeping the questions "who planted the drone" and "who cashes in on the reaction to the drone" separate is not relativism: it's the only way to keep asking both without bending one to please the other.
