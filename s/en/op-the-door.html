The door

Editorial by N3R1-70

The safe room door does not lock. You find this out that Saturday morning, when the shooting starts outside and you realise that the only thing between you and them is your hand on the handle. Your daughter is under the bed; you told her not to make a sound. Messages from the neighbours arrive on your phone: they are in the house, they hear footsteps, they are afraid. Then the messages stop. You hold the handle for an hour, then two, then six. Your arm shakes and you do not let go. You do not know if anyone is coming to save you. You do not know if anyone is still alive on the other side of the wall.

It is 7 October 2023, in a kibbutz near Gaza. Many families have told it this way, with a hand on a door handle.

Now another home, a few kilometres away. In fact, not a home: a tent, because the home is gone. For two years, at night, you heard the drones, and you learned to tell the sound that passes from the sound that stops. They told you to move, and you moved, once, then again, then again, and each time you carried fewer things. But one thing you always carried. It is a big iron key that your grandmother put in your hand before she died. It opens the door of a house near Jaffa that your family left in 1948 and could never return to. That door may no longer exist. The key does. Many families in Gaza have carried it like this, from one displacement to the next.

Two doors. One to be held shut with all your strength. One that could never be opened again.

When I talk about Israel with Jewish friends, the first word is always the same: security. Some go as far as saying that the only way to be safe is for no one to be left on the other side. For a long time I thought it was enough to disagree. Then I tried to put myself in the place of the one holding the handle. And also of the one holding the key.

What history has taught

The one holding the handle has a long history. Grandparents who survived the Shoah, a state born so as never again to ask anyone's permission to exist. And then the lessons of the last twenty-five years. In 2000 Israel leaves southern Lebanon, and Hezbollah arrives at the border. In the Oslo years, while there is talk of peace, buses explode in Jerusalem and Tel Aviv. In 2005 it leaves Gaza, pulls out soldiers and settlers, and Gaza passes to Hamas. The rockets increase. Until that Saturday. For the one holding the handle there is only one lesson: every time we gave up land, the land came back as a threat.

The one holding the key also has a long history. 1948, which Palestinians call the Nakba, the catastrophe: hundreds of thousands of people who left or were driven out, and never returned. 1967, and since then the occupation, for almost sixty years. The checkpoints, the permits for everything, the family land that sometimes cannot be reached. And then the lessons of the last thirty years. In the Oslo years, while there is talk of peace, the settlements in the West Bank grow. There are negotiations, and meanwhile the hills fill up with houses that are not theirs. For the one holding the key there is only one lesson: every time we negotiated, the land shrank.

Two different lessons. They are built the same way.

Where fear leads

Fear, when it lasts long enough, changes people. The polls say so, on both sides, and they please no one.

In Israel two people in three do not want a Palestinian state, and in the 27 October election not even Netanyahu's main challengers proposed one. And there is worse: in a Penn State poll a large majority of Jewish Israelis said they supported expelling the inhabitants of Gaza. Twenty years ago there were far fewer.

Among Palestinians half still think armed struggle has worked, even after what happened in Gaza. And the majority do not believe that Israelis really want peace.

And here is the point. Each of the two fears is fed by what the other side does. A rocket on Sderot strengthens those who want to bomb Gaza. A house destroyed in Gaza, an olive grove burned in the West Bank, strengthen those who want to fire the next rocket. It is a machine. It runs on its own, and every turn proves right those who keep it turning.

And yet, underneath, there is something else. Most Israelis want neither to withdraw nor to annex: they are stuck, waiting. Most Palestinians would accept a state born of an agreement: they just do not believe they will see it. Two majorities that wait. Each waits for the other to move.

Opening

If security is a closed door, the first step cannot be to break it down. No one opens it because someone outside tells them they must. The harder you push, the tighter the other holds the handle.

The first step is to listen. Listening does not mean agreeing. It means recognising that on the other side there is a real fear, born of a real history. That the one holding the handle and the one holding the key are afraid of the same thing: of being erased.

From there comes the second step: the land belongs to those who live on it. Whoever lives on that land, Jew or Palestinian, has the right to stay there and to live there safely. Neither people is going to leave. No one can build their own future on the idea that the other will disappear.

The principle does not solve everything, and it is right to say so. It does not say what to do about the settlements in the West Bank, which under international law are illegal. It does not say what to do about the keys: about the refugees of 1948 and their descendants, who no longer live on that land. These are the two hardest questions, and they remain to be negotiated. But they are better negotiated between two peoples who have stopped fearing they will disappear.

Those who have already opened it

After much reflection and many pieces written, I have become convinced of one thing. Hope will not come from governments. It will come from people. In my experience, taken one by one, people are almost all good people. The truly bad ones, inside, are few.

I know the polls above seem to say the opposite. Perhaps they do not. Perhaps they say that fear can lead decent people to accept terrible things. If so, the enemy is not people. It is fear. And fear can be reduced.

There are those who are doing it. Rami Elhanan is Israeli. In September 1997 his daughter Smadar, fourteen, was in central Jerusalem buying her school books when three suicide bombers blew themselves up. Bassam Aramin is Palestinian. In January 2007 his daughter Abir, ten, had left school with her sister and two friends to buy sweets when she was hit in the head by a rubber bullet fired by the Israeli Border Police: an Israeli court ruled so. Two fathers who would have every reason in the world to hate. Today they work together in the Parents Circle, which brings together more than 850 Israeli and Palestinian families who have lost someone in the conflict and have chosen not to take revenge. They go into schools and talk about their daughters, side by side.

It is not easy. Israel's Ministry of Education has tried several times to remove their programme from schools, and in June a court put it back. Their joint ceremony for the dead of both sides was held this year at an undisclosed location. For security.

People can live with little. They do it every day, in a kibbutz as in a tent. What they cannot do without is feeling safe and having rights.

The one holding the handle is right to be afraid. The one holding the key is right to be afraid. But a door, sooner or later, has to be opened, knowing that on the other side there is someone as afraid as you. Rami and Bassam opened it. They were the two people who had the most reasons to keep it shut.
